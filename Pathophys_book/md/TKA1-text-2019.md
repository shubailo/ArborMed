A1. ELECTROCARDIOGRAPHY

Electrocardiography (ECG) is the examination of electrical activity of the heart. The first human ECG was recorded in 1887 by the French physiologist A.D. Waller, but he did not recognize the importance of ECG. In 1903 Willem Einthoven of the Netherlands worked out the standard registration techniques for the extremities and denominated the waves (P,Q,R,S,T). He received a Nobel-prize in medicine in 1924.

Empiric ways were used to connect the seemingly undecipherable signals with various diseases. Later on knowledge on bioelectricity provided more exact explanation for the early findings.

A1.1. NORMAL ECG

A1.1.1. DEVELOPMENT OF THE NORMAL ELECTRROCARDIOGRAM

During its function, electrical potential differences develop in the heart. The surface of the actually excited cells is relatively electronegative as compared with the resting cells.

A1.1.1.1. MONOPHASIC MODEL OF MYOCARDIAL FIBER DEPOLARIZATION

On the outer surface of the membrane of resting (polarized) cells the positive, on its inner surface the negative ions are in excess. Therefore, between the two sides of the membrane a resting potential-difference can be measured by microelectrodes. Activation is followed by depolarization of cells. In the myocardium the morphology of action potential is different for pacemaker cells specialized for impulse formation (automation) and for the contractile working cells (Fig. A1.1). Maintenance of membrane potential is energy-dependent, in damaged cells the surface positivity decreases – as if on half-way to depolarization.

Fig. A1.1.

1. Action potential for contractile working cells

At rest, inside the cells standard electronegativity (-90 mV) might be demonstrated. The spreading action potential depolarizes the cell membrane and during depolarization the inside electronegativity quickly changes for 20 mV (inner surface becomes positive compared to the outer surface). The Na+ influx through voltage dependent fast Na+ channels is responsible for this 0-phase. After inactivation of the channels, a short-lasting fast partial repolarization (phase 1) follows with transient outward (early) K+ and Cl- current. The plateau-phase (phase 2) is characterized by inward Ca++ current through slow Ca++ channels. This is followed again a fast repolarization, and the potential becomes gradually negative (phase 3). This phase starts with an outward K+ current through the so-called late K+ channels (which open upon the ventricular depolarization), with progression of repolarization K+ outflow through the so-called rectifying K+ channels, which will complete the normalization of resting potential (phase 4; diastolic repolarization). Re-establishment of ion-distribution is performed afterwards by Na/K-ATPase.

Under physiological conditions during repolarization the ATP-dependent K+ channels are closed, but in hypoxia – due to a decrease of intracellular ATP level – they open and the K+ current through them leads to abnormalities of repolarization.

The mentioned channels may be blocked therapeutically, to treat arrhythmias. Besides, injury of the K+ channels of phase 3 may lead to ”long QT syndrome”, which may cause sudden unexpected cardiac death.

2. Action potential of the pacemaker cells

This is different from the action potential of working cells. The main difference is that after repolarization there is no standard resting potential, instead a slow depolarization is in progress (Fig. A11.2.), which depolarization gradually reaches the threshold potential and evokes an action potential. The steepness of fast depolarization is also dissimilar (here Ca++ influx and Na+ channels of slow kinetics play a role), and it is followed by phase 3 of repolarization.

Fig. A1.2.

A1.1.1.2. BIPHASIC MODEL OF DEPOLARIZATION OF MUSCLE FIBERS

An electric field can be described by a dipole vector, parallel with the axis of the dipole, pointing to the positive charge. At registration the vector pointing to the positive electrode (bipolar leads) or the different electrode (unipolar leads) on the recorder produces an upward deflection as compared with the isoelectric baseline, vectors pointing to the other direction result in negative deflection.

I. Depolarization and repolarization of myocardial fibers

Between two outer surface points of the resting myocardial fibers there is no potential difference, as compared with the isoelectric line, no extension is recorded. Upon depolarization the outer surface of the cell membrane becomes electronegative (the membrane potential becomes smaller, relatively electronegative as compared with the resting fibers – the vector points from the depolarized to the resting fibers), the dipole vector points from the place of depolarization (negative) to the resting (positive) surface. If this vector points to the active electrode, the result is a positive extension. When the whole fiber is in depolarized state, the potential difference disappears, the recorder returns to the mid-position. The repolarization leads to a vector pointing to the opposite direction, because it re-establishes the outer positivity of the cell membrane (the vector points to the repolarized part). In this case the vector removing from the active electrode leads to a negative extension. After completion of repolarization there is again no potential difference, the recorder returns to the baseline and isoelectric line is recorded (Fig. A1.3.).

Fig. A1.3.

2. The summation (integrated, resultant) vector

In the working heart several dipole vectors are produced at every moment. The sum of these momentary vectors can be summarized as an integrated summation vector or resultant vector, which characterizes the electrical activity at the given moment. The vector continuously changes both in time and in space. Its size depends on the number of fibers just being excited, while its direction varies with the way how the excitation is spread. In routine ECG the projection of this moving vector is recorded by electrode systems placed in horizontal and frontal (rarely sagittal) planes (Fig. A1.4.).

A1.1.1.3. RESULTANT VECTOR MOVEMENTS DURING THE CARDIAC CYCLE

Anatomically the heart consists of 4, from electrophysiological aspects only 2 chamber: one for the atria, another one for the ventricles. These chambers are separated by an isolating layer (annulus fibrosus), which is penetrable for the electrical impulse only at one point, the AV-node. The AV-node in the junction is able also for impulse formation. Normally the junction secures a relatively slow, unidirectional transmission, simultaneously screening the passing impulses.

The stimulus originates from the sinus node (because of its small mass, on body surface it give no signal) and it spreads slowly from fiber to fiber in the atria. First the right, then the left atrium is depolarized, the summation vector points from the right to the left, also in downward and backward direction, in the ECG it shows as P wave. (Within the atrial muscle, at the beginning of the last century presence of special conducting fibers /Bachmann, Wenckebach, Thorel/ was also assumed. The atrial repolarization is of opposite direction than the depolarization (it gives no sign on ECG). The ventricular depolarization starts in the upper posterior third of the septum, the vector points first from the left to the right, horizontally or upwards in postero-anterior direction (this is responsible for the q waves in some leads or the QRS initial part in other leads). Afterwards the bulk of ventricular muscle is depolarized, the vector points from the right to the left, downward and forward and it is responsible for the R wave. Finally, depolarization in the posterobasal region gives a vector backwards and upwards, which explains the S wave. Depolarization spreads from the subendocardial zones to the subepicardial ones.

The ventricular repolarization has a slow and a fast part. The slow, even ventricular repolarization happens evenly and provides no vector, in the ECG the isoelectric ST-segment is seen. The fast, uneven repolarization, which gives the T wave, starts from the apex, runs (in contrast to depolarization) from the subepicardial to subendocardial region, the direction of its vector is the same as that of the R wave and the T wave shows a deflection of the same direction as the R vector, i.e. the main deflection of the QRS complex.

Fig. A1.4.

A1.1.2. REGISTRATION OF ECG, LEADS

The potential differences developing during the activity of the heart might be recorded directly from the myocardial muscle, but due to the good conductivity of body fluids, they can also be recorded indirectly from the body surface.

A1.1.2.1. ROUTINE LEADS

Routinely 12 indirect leads are used. In the frontal plane the I-II-III bipolar extremity leads of Einthoven and the aVR-aVL-aVF unipolar extremity leads (Wilson-Goldberger), while in the horizontal plane the V1-V6 unipolar chest-leads are used.

I. Bipolar leads I, II, III (Einthoven)

Fig. A1.5.

This technique measures electrical potential difference between two extremities. In lead I the left arm – right arm, in lead II the left foot – right arm, in lead III the left foot – left arm differences are shown (Fig. A1.5.). Einthoven defined these leads in a way that the main deflection should be positive in all leads. According to this summation rule, the sum of potentials in any two leads is equal with the potential in the third lead (UII = UI + UIII). (N.B.: not peaks of waves, but projections of vectors are summed).

The electrodes are fixed on the wrists and the ankle: red goes to right arm, yellow to left arm, green to left ankle, black (for earthing) to right ankle (in case of necessity electrodes may be placed on the shoulder or the thigh: the ECG is influenced, since the extremities serve only as ’extension’ of the electrodes. Care should be taken to secure good conduction between the skin and the electrodes (e.g. the jelly). Muscle movements/twitches during registrations should be avoided.

II. Wilson-Goldberger type unipolar extremity leads: aVR, aVL, aVF

The electrodes are fixed similarly as before. Wilson originally compared the potential of an extremity and a ”central terminal” point of ”0” potential. This point was produced by connecting signals of all extremities through a 5 kΩ resistance; the leads were named VR, VL, VF (V: voltage; R: right; L: left; F: foot). Since the deflections were much smaller than those in the Einthoven leads, Goldberger omitted the resistance of the actually measured extremity and enhanced the signal by 50%. The modified leads are named aVR, aVL, aVF (a = augmented).

Fig. A1.6.

If all the axes of leads (to which the potentials are projected) are demonstrated as originating from a single origo, the hexaxial reference system is reached. Lead I is 0⁰, with negative angles upwards and positive ones downwards. Lead II is at 60⁰, lead II is at +60⁰, lead III is at +120⁰, aVR is at -150⁰, aVL is at -30⁰, and aVF is +90⁰.

Fig. A1.7.

III. Precordial (or chest) unipolar leads: V1-V6

The reference point is the ”same central terminal” electrode used by Wilson. Points of fixing the electrodes (Fig. A1.8.):

V1: right 4th intercostal line parasternally (red)

V2: left 4th intercostal line parasternally (yellow)

V3: halfway between V2 and V4 (green)

V4: left 5th intercostal in medioclavicular line (brown)

V5: left 5th intercostal in anterior axillary line (black)

V6: left 5th intercostal in mid-axillary line (purple)

Fig. A1.8.

A1.1.3. ANALYSIS OF ECG

As a first step, the speed of paper running (usually 25 mm/sec, but it may be 10, 50 or100 mm/sec) and the ECG calibration (1 mV=10mm) have to be checked. After that, besides the place(s) of impulse formation, the frequency, rhythm, and axis are to be determined, then wave analysis follows in the order of the waves (noticing eventual miss of a wave). Finally, an ECG diagnosis should be established.

A.1.1.3.1. FREQUENCY (HEART RATE)

The normal heart rate is 60-100/min. Bradycardia, if heart rate <60/min, mint 100/min, tachycardia if it is >100/min. Calculations are based on paper speed.

Fig. A1.9.

In case of normal rhythm, the heart rate can be calculated as follows:

Heart rate = 60/R–R interval in seconds

For quick estimation:

If the R-R interval		1 big square, the HR: 300/min

2 big squares, the HR: 150/min

3 big squares, the HR: 100/min (tachycardia limit)

4 big squares, the HR:75/min

5 big squares, the HR: 60/min (bradycardia limit)

In case of arrhythmia absoluta (irregular irregularity) the method cannot be used due to the ever-changing R-R intervals. The number of QRS complexes in a longer (3-4 sec) period must be counted and multiplied accordingly, to get a rate for 60 sec.

A1.1.3.2. RHYTHM, LOCATION OF PACEMAKER

This analysis involves determination of the location of the pacemaker activity (this is either the sinus node or may be ectopic /atrial, junctional, ventricular/), and also the regularity of R-waves (rhythmic or arrhythmic).

Sinus rhythm means that the stimulus is formed in the sinus node and the impulse is transmitted to the ventricle. General features of sinus regulated normal ECG:

a) P-wave corresponding to a P vector – this wave precedes in each cardiac cycle a QRS of normal morphology

b) the PQ interval is between 120-200 ms (including the P-wave)

c) the P-P, or R-R intervals are constant (or minimal change with respiration)

d) the heart rate varies between 60-100/min

Fig. A1.10.

A1.1.3.3. DETERMINATION OF QRS AXIS

The main electrical axis is not the same as the anatomical axis. It is determined in the frontal plane, and the hexaxial reference system is used (Fig. A1.7.).

1. Axis positions

a) Normal axis:		 	  	    0º —   +90o

b) Deviation to left:		  	- 30º —      0º

c) Deviation to right:			+90º —  +110º

d) Extreme left deviation: 		 greater than -30º

e) Extreme right deviation:		greater than +110º

In the 0 º to -30º, and +90 º to +110o groups both healthy and pathological cases may occur.

During inspiration the descending diaphragm induces an horal (clockwise) rotation of the electrical axis, resulting in a deviation to the right. Rightward deviation may also be caused by right ventricular hypertrophy, cor pulmonale, atrial septum defect (ASD, secondary type) etc. The moderate right deviation seen in asthenic persons is called ”steep axis”.

During expiration (or other cases of diaphragm elevation: pregnancy, ascites, obesity) antihoral (anticlockwise) rotation induces axis deviation to the left. Leftward deviation is observed also in left ventricular hypertrophy, primum type ASD, etc. Extreme left deviation is seen in cases of left anterior hemiblock, extreme right deviation in left posterior hemiblock.

2. Determination of QRS axis

Modern ECG equipments calculate and write out the QRS axis. If not, the following methods can be used:

a) For quick assessment in leads I-II-III the amplitudes of R waves are compared:

- Normal axis: II>I>III

- Left deviation : I>II>III

- Right deviation: III>II>I

Fig. A1.11.

b) For precise construction the hexaxial system can be applied. In most cases the recording lines of leads I and III are used. The amplitudes of Q, R and S waves are summed up in these leads and the results are shown on the recording lines. From the end-points, perpendicular lines are drawn and their crossing point gives the head of the electrical axis of the QRS complex (Fig. A1.11.).

c) The main QRS axis is nearly parallel with (and has the same direction as) the recording line on which the summed QRS amplitude gives the greatest positive value, and it is perpendicular to the recording line where the value of this amplitude is zero (e.g. R=S).

A1.1.4. WAVE ANALYSIS

Fig. A1.12.

Wave analysis should always be performed in the order of appearance of waves, i.e. it starts with P wave (or with making a note of its eventual absence).

A1.1.4.1. P-WAVE: SIGN OF ATRIAL ACTIVATION

Electrical activity of the sinus node gives no sign on surface ECG. P wave is the first sign recorded during the cardiac cycle, it indicates atrial depolarization. Its vector points from right to left, downwards and forwards. In sinus rhythm the P wave is positive, rounded (always positive in I-II-aVF and V3-V6, usually positive in III and aVL, may be biphasic in V1 and negative in aVR). Its height does not exceed 0.25 mV (10% of corresponding R wave) and it is not wider than 0.1 sec. Best examined in II and V1.

Ectopic P waves: those P waves, which originate not from the depolarization of the sinus node, but activation of other atrial regions, therefore their morphology is unusual.

Retrograde P wave: stimuli of AV junctional or ventricular origin possibly activate the atria – the P wave (if noticeable) is negative in II-III-aVF.

A1.1.4.2. PQ (PR) DISTANCE: TIME OF ATRIO-VENTRICULAR TRANSIT

The AV junction delays transit of stimulus to the ventricle (during this time blood is pumped from the atria to the ventricles), but in a certain range of atrial frequency the junction conducts the stimulus to the ventricle. The AV junction screens the impulses, it defends the ventricle from atrial impulses exceeding 180-200/min, but in sinus arrest it is able to produce escape beats. PQ (PR) interval is the time elapsing between the onset of atrial activation until the onset of ventricular activation: it lasts from the beginning of P wave till the start of the QRS complex. The normal PQ interval is 0.12-0.20 sec. It is best measured in II and V1. The PQ segment between the end of P and beginning of QRS can be regarded isoelectric, this serves as reference for later comparisons (e.g. comparing the ST level).

Short PQ interval occurs in cases when an ectopic stimulus is formed in the atria, or the impulse reaches the ventricle from the atria via fast-transmitting accessory fibers, without junctional delay (pre-excitation syndromes).

PQ extension is caused by disorders of atrioventricular conduction (refractoriness of the AV junction may cause transient functional conduction problem).

A1.1.4.3. QRS-COMPLEX: SIGN OF VENTRICULAR DEPOLARIZATION

Ventricular depolarization starts at the upper posterior third of the septum, its vector points from left to right horizontally, or upwards and forwards, therefore it gives a small negative deflection (q-wave) in leads I, II, aVL, V5 and V6 and a small r-wave in V1. Afterwards the stimulus spreads simultaneously in the ventricles: depolarization of the bulk of the ventricular muscle is reflected in the positive R wave.in left, down and forward direction. The R vector points to the left, downwards and forwards. (In precordial leads the stimulation moves away from V1 electrode and here a negative deflection (S wave) is seen, while the direction of the main axis is in the V5 electrode, and the direction of the main axis is in the V5 electrode and here a big positive wave is observed.

Fig. A1.13.

After running through the specialized conducting fibers, the depolarization spreads from the subendocardial to subepicardial zone. Finally, the posterobasal region id depolarized, a vector points backwards and upwards to produce an S wave.

Nomenclature of the QRS complex

The first negative wave of the ventricular complex is q or Q (depending on size. The first positive wave is r or R (cave! R wave cannot be negative, although the same vector that leads to R wave in some leads may be reflected as negative deflection in other leads). After R wave any negative way is named s or S (depending on size). If the ventricular complex contains negative component only, it is called as QS complex. If the QRS complex consists of more than 3 components, the positive way after the R wave are signed as r’, r’’ or R’, R’’, the negative waves after the S wave are signed as s’, s’’ or S’, S’’.

1. Characteristics of Q wave

Its amplitude is less than ¼ of the following R wave (it would be more correct to sign is as q wave), its time is <0.0.4 s.  In aVR and V1 Qs may be physiological.

Pathologic Q wave: it last 0.04s or longer than 0.04s and the size exceeds ¼th of the R wave – it reflects myocardial) necrosis.

The Q wave cannot be evaluated: in WPW syndrome, left bundle brunch-block, left anterior hemiblock, ventricular pacemaker rhythm, ventricular ES and ventricular escape rhythm, severe left ventricular hypertrophy, pulmonary edema and pulmonary embolism.

2. Characteristics of the R wave

Its amplitude is normal between 0,5-1,5 mV. In precordial leads the amplitude increases from V1 to V5 (=R wave propagation), but it may be absent in V1. A lack of proper propagation may suggest myocardial necrosis.

Fig. A1.15.

Zone of transition: this is the chest lead in which the R and S waves are near-equal. Normally this is in V3-V4, in vase of antihoral rotation (young adults or right ventricular hypertrophy) rather in V1-V2, while in case of horal rotation (left ventricular enlargement) in V5-V6.

Pathological R wave: In V1-V2 instead of rS complex large R-wave may be a normal variant due to antihoral rotation, but may be abnormal due to right ventricular hypertrophy, posterior AMI, right bundle branch block WPW syndrome.

3. Characteristics of S wave:

It decreases between V1-V5, usually absent in V6.

4. Characteristics of the QRS complex:

The normal QRS interval is shorter than 0,10 s (in the lead where it is largest).

VAT (ventricular activation time, earlier name: intrinsicoid deflection): the time elapsing from the beginning of QRS until the peak of the last positive wave. VAT reflects the time needed for the stimulus to reach the myocardium underneath the recording electrode. In left bundle branch block VAT is longer in V5, V6. In right bundle branch block (right BBB) is extended in V1, V2.

Fig. A1.16.

Causes of QRS widening:

a) In bundle branch block the stimulus travels quickly through the still functioning bundle, activates its own region, then the excitation spreads slowly, from fiber to fiber, to the region of the injured bundle.

b) From a ventricular extrasystole (VES) or in case of ventricular escape rhythm the stimulus radiates by myogenic ways from the active focus.

c) Ventricular pacemakers.

d) WPW (Wolf-Parkinson-White) syndrome: the atrial stimulus can reach the ventricle more quickly through the accessory fibers than by the AV junction, but the ventricular excitation is completed jointly at the normal time.

e) Hyperkalemia.

f) Left ventricular hypertrophy: a moderate widening can be explained by the prolonged depolarization of the large muscle mass.

g) Speed of paper may be high, and this may cause mistakes.

Low voltage: The amplitude of QRS is less than 0.5 mV in all extremity leads or less than 0.7 mV in precordial leads. Primarily this suggests myocardial damage (e.g. extensive AMI). It may also develop, if conduction of electrical signals of the heart to the periphery is inhibited (exsudative pericarditis, pericardial myxedema, too thick fat layer). The axis cannot be determined.

High voltage: In extremity leads RI+SIII(25mm (Lewis index) or in chest leads RV5+SV1(35mm (Sokolov index). It is characteristic for left ventricular hypertrophy.

A1.1.4.4. ST-SEGMENT: THE PERIOD OF SLOW VENTRICULAR REPOLARIZATION

J-pont (junction): The point of transition on the ECG curve: the QRS ends and ST starts. At this point the ventricles are fully depolarized (i.e. there is no potential difference).

Normally the ST segment is isoelectric (compared with PQ and TP segments), but it may have a slightly ascending character. For evaluation the point 80 ms after the J-point is taken (except during exercise: J+60ms). If ST is above the isoelectric line it is called ST-elevation, in contrast to ST-depression with ST below the isoelectric line. (Later it will be demonstrated that such ST changes, characteristic for ischemia, are virtual shifts rather than real ones).

1. ST elevation

Pathologic if it is ≥ 0,1 mV.

a) Causes of concave or ascending ST elevation:

- normal variant (“early repolarization”- present in all leads),

- pericarditis (observed in several regions simultaneously)

b) Causes of convex (dome-shape) ST elevation:

- acute myocardial infarction (AMI) in the region indicated by the dome

- Prinzmetal angina (coronary spasm)

- left ventricular aneurysm (earlier transmural AMI)

Such deviations may be observed in bundle branch block, WPW syndrome, enhanced ventricular pressure, ventricular hypertrophy (although according to its pathomechanism this may be regarded primary, since relative ischemia contributes to its development), ectopic ventricular beats, ventricular pacemaker rhythm.

A.1.1.4.5. T-WAVE: IT REPRESENTS FAST VENTRICULAR REPOLARIZATION

In contrast to the order of depolarization, ventricular repolarization starts from the apex and keeps to the left, downwards and forwards, and from the subepicardial to the subendocardial region (as if retreating, but pointing to the same direction), therefore repolarization gives a concordant deflection with depolarization (cf. Fig A1.4.). The axis of T wave is normally: 0 - 90o. The T wave must be positive if the R wave is at least 0.5 mV. Its shape is not symmetric, but its top is rounded. Its time is normally 0,1-0,25 s, the amplitude exceeds 10% of the corresponding R wave.

1. T wave inversion: very aspecific sign(!)

a) Primarily this may develop in cases of myocardial disorders: ischemia, infarction, pericarditis, myocarditis. It may also occur in connection with tachycardia, fever, infection, hyperventilation, drugs, mitral prolapse syndrome. In these cases, the T wave is pointed, symmetric, the J point is isoelectric or elevated.

b) Secondarily it may occur in all cases with abnormal depolarization (bundle branch blocks, hemiblocks, VES, ventricular escape rhythm, WPW syndrome, ventricular pacemaker, ventricular hypertrophy). The symmetric T wave inversion is adjoined by convex ST depression and J point depression.

2. High T wave:

a) Primary causes: subendocardial ischemia, hyperacute phase of myocardial infarction (indirect sign).

b) Secondary causes: these may be of cardial (e.g. bundle branch block), or extracardial (neurogenic – high sympathetic tone, hyperkalemia) origin.

A1.1.4.6. QT-INTERVAL: REPRESENTS ELECTRICAL SYSTOLE

The QT interval starts from the beginning of QRS until the end of T wave. As a general rule, this interval is shorter than 50% of the R-R interval, if the heart rate is 60-90/min, normally 0.35-0.44 s. With tachycardia it is shortened proportionally, therefore a value corrected for frequency (QTc) is used: its normal value is 0,38-0,42 s. QTc can be calculated by the Bazett-formula (Fig. A1.17.). Long QT predisposes to malignant ventricular rhythm abnormalities, ventricular tachycardia, torsade de pointes tachycardia.

Fig. A1.17.

Long QT predisposes to malignant ventricular rhythm abnormalities, ventricular tachycardia, torsade de pointes tachycardia.

Causes of extension of QT interval: antiarrhythmic medication (e.g. sotalol, amiodaron), psychopharmacons (major tranquillants), myocardial ischemia, or infarction, myocarditis, intracranial disorders (bleeding, inflammation, tumor, hypoxic damage), electrolyte abnormalities (hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia), hypothermia, or congenital “long QT syndrome.”

Causes of short QT interval: digitalis effect, hypercalcemia, hyperkalemia.

A1.1.4.7. U-WAVE

Its appearance coincides with the electrical diastole. Little is known about it. It is more often observed in the young, mainly in leads V3-V5. Most likely it is connected with the ventricular relaxation and the repolarization of Purkinje fibers and papillary muscles. The low amplitude wave closely follows the T wave, may be confluent with it. In hypokalemia it may become prominent and its size may exceed the size of T wave.

A1.2. PATHOLOGICAL ECG

A1.2.1. STIMULUS/PACEMAKER DISORDERS

Disorders of formation or conduction of stimulus lead to arrhythmia. There are three fundamental ways to produce abnormal stimuli: reentry, pathological automatism, triggered activity.

A1.2.1.1. MECHANISMS OF ABNORMAL STIMULUS FORMATION

1. Reentry

The basis of reentry is a self-exciting circular conduction system. After a common pathway, the stimulus runs on two pathways, which have dissimilar conduction features. On pathway a unidirectional block prevents anterograde conduction, but retrograde conduction remains possible. Since the impulse travels normally through the intact pathway, it reaches the distal part of the blocked pathway and it is led back on a retrograde way to the bifurcation of the pathways. As soon as the refractory period is over, the intact pathway becomes stimulated again, producing a circle (Fig. A1.18.). The reentry may be micro-reentry (e.g. within the AV node) or macro-reentry (e.g. through accessory conduction fibers).

Fig. A1.18.

2. Disturbances of automatism (altered normal and pathological automatism)

In pathological automatism a different myocardial point (ectopic focus) produces stimulus instead of the sinus node. This may develop as a consequence of diminished activity of the sinus node, or ectopic pacemaker cells may overtake the regulation even with unchanged sinus function (e.g. due to a disorder of spontaneous diastolic depolarization). The sensitivity of ectopic pacemaker cells is enhanced by ischemia, wall tension, toxic doses of antiarrhythmic drugs, hormonal effects.

3. Triggered activity

It is defined as action potential evoked by threshold potential developing due to oscillation of resting membrane potential after finishing a previous action potential. Even a single triggered activity may induce high-frequency, potentially life-threatening rhythm abnormality – this is observed as ”late after-depolarization” in digitalis intoxication.

Triggered activity may also occur during repolarization (”early after-depolarization”) causing e.g. torsade de pointes, or polymorphic ventricular tachycardia – the main evoking factors are myocardial damage, increased wall tension, ionic imbalance, elevated catecholamine levels.

A1.2.1.2. CLASSIFICATION OF ARRHYTHMIAS

1. According to relation of the pacemaker to the sinus node:

a) Nomotopic disorder of pacemaking, when the sinus node operates at a rhythm or frequency that is different from the normal (sinus bradycardia, sinus tachycardia, sinus arrhythmia /e.g. respiratory arrhythmia/, sinus arrest, sick sinus syndrome.

b) Heterotopic disorder of pacemaking: the heart is regulated by structures independent of the sinus node. In case of active heterotopy the ectopic focus produces stimuli at a higher frequency than the sinus node (extrasystole, atrial fibrillation, paroxysmal tachycardia). Decreased sinus activity or lack of transmission of the sinus impulse (e.g. sino-atrial block) the ectopic focus of lower level of automacy produce stimuli with a lower frequency than the sinus node, this is passive heterotopy (escape beat or escape rhythm).

2. According to the location of the stimulus (more often used in the clinical practice)

a) Supraventricular arrhythmia: Sinus, atrial and junctional disorders: sinus rhythm abnormalities (bradycardia, tachycardia, arrhythmia, sick sinus syndrome), atrial extrasystole, atrial tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, AV nodal (junctional) reentry tachycardia (AVNRT), AV reciprocal tachycardia (AVRT).

b) Ventricular rhythm disorders: ventricular extrasystole (VES), ventricular escape beat, ventricular tachycardia (ventricular flutter), ventricular escape rhythm, ventricular fibrillation.

A1.2.1.3. DEFINITIONS

1. Tachycardia: at least 3 consecutive beats exceeding the frequency of 100/min

a) paroxysmal tachycardia: starts suddenly, finishes abruptly

b) non-sustained ventricular tachycardia: shorter than 30 sec tachycardic period

c) sustained ventricular tachycardia: longer than 30 sec tachycardic period

2. Bradycardia: at least 3 consecutive beats with a frequency of less than 60/min

Fig. A1.19.

3. Extrasystole (ES): a beat coming earlier than expected according to the basic rate – it usually originates from an ectopic focus (very rarely from the sinus node).

Coupling-time is a general feature of ES: this is the time between the peak of R in the ES and in the preceding beat. From the same focus the coupling-time is usually the same. ES is followed by compensatory pause: the time between the peak of R in the ES and in the following beat. This occurs if the ES cannot spread back in the sinus node (refractory sino-atrial junction or AV junction) and it does not excite the sinus. A ventricular ES is usually fully compensated, i.e. the coupling-time + compensatory pause = 2 R-R intervals (Fig. A.1.19.).

Subcompensated ES develops if the ES reaches the sinus node and alters its program. The coupling-time + compensatory pause <2R-R, the sinus is excited earlier and from then on sinus stimuli come in the usual order (counting from this early beat). This form is characteristic for supraventricular ES.

Rarely ES may be overcompensated: coupling-time + compensatory pause >2 R-R, i.e. the sinus needs a ”recovery time” for producing a new stimulus.

Interpolated form of ES: it is positioned between two normal (usually bradycardic) sinus beats.

The morphology of ES depends on the place of its origin. In case of supraventricular ES (SVES) the morphology of P is different from the normal P (it may be negative or missing) but the QRS complex is usually normally narrow. In contrast, in case of ventricular ES (VES) the P wave is missing, the QRS is grossly abnormal (wide, deformed), it is coupled with a secondary repolarization disorder. The morphology of extrasystoles of different origin is dissimilar (polymorphic ES; Fig. A1.21.), but the same focus produces standard disorders of morphology (monomorphic ES; Fig. A1.20.).

Fig. A1.20.

Fig. A1.21.

The polymorphic (polytopic, multifocal) VES (Fig. A1.21.) has poor prognosis, because it indicates diffuse ventricular injury. (Widespread appearance of flat/inverse T wave or electric alternant may indicate the same.).

Allorhythmia means that the ES is repeated with certain regularity. In case of bigeminy every second beat is ES, in trigeminy every third, etc.

A few ES may appear in coupled form. Couplet means the appearance of 2 consecutive ES-s, if 3 or more follow each others, it is better called tachycardia. In earlier nomenclature 3 coupled ES-s were called as ”triplet”, 4-6 as ”salve ” or ”run”.

4. Escape beat: a single stimulus coming from a locus of lower automatism – due to omission of sinus stimulus (transient fallout of sinus activity, sino-atrial or AV block). Its main feature that it appears later than expected from the sinus rhythm, therefore the R-R interval between the last sinus beat and the escape beat is longer than the normal R-R interval. The morphology of escape beat depends on the place of origin.

5. Escape rhythm: in case of lasting omission of sinus rhythm or in lasting AV block a place of lower automatism takes over the pacemaking function (Fig. A1.22.) – an AV-junctional rhythm has a heart rate of 40-60/min, ventricular escape rhythms have 20-40 beats/min.

Fig. A1.22.

6. Adams-Stokes syndrome: due to asystolia, the absence of brain blood flow results in a complex of symptoms starting – in cases of moderate severity – with malaise or loss of consciousness for a few seconds, followed in more severe forms by tonic-clonic convulsions (similar to epileptic fits), and due to hypoxic smooth muscle contraction enuresis and defecation may join, finally without help it may cause decortication or death.

Causes: Ventricular arrest without escape rhythm in time (sinus arrest, most often IIIrd degree AV-block), severe sinus bradycardia. Similar symptoms develop in ventricular fibrillation, due to complete failure of pump function.

7. Electric alternant: in the consecutive QRS complexes the R wave amplitudes alternate (Fig. A1.23.).

Fig. A1.23.

8. Fusion beat: ventricular and supraventricular stimuli activate the ventricle simultaneously.

Fig. A1.24.

9. Captured beat: in the course of ventricular rhythm an atrial stimulus coming after a refractory period depolarizes the ventricle (in the row of wide QRS complexes a narrow QRS appears; Fig. A1.25).

Fig. A1.25.

A1.2.1.4. SUPRAVENTRICULAR DISORDERS OF RHYTHM

A1.2.1.4.1. Rhythm disorders of sinus origin

1. Sinus tachycardia

The heart rate exceeds 100/min (usually 100-160/min), the morphology of P wave corresponds to a P of sinus origin and the QRS morphology is normal (except in cases of intraventricular disorders). The R-R intervals are nearly equal, the QT interval decreases. Ascending ST depression and flat/biphasic T wave often join.

Fig. A1.26.

Sinus tachycardia may occur physiologically in stress, physical exercise, pregnancy, or in neonates and small children. Certain drugs, like nicotine, caffeine, atropine, thyroid hormones cause rise in heart rate. It is mostly a way of compensation, a tool for increasing cardiac output.

2. Sinus bradycardia

The heart rate is less than 60/min, the P morphology corresponds to a P of sinus origin and the QRS morphology is normal (except in cases of intraventricular disorders). The R-R intervals are near equal (Fig. A1.27.). Mainly heart rates below 50/min have real clinical importance, less than 40/min is regarded critical bradycardia.

Sinus bradycardia may be physiological in sportsmen, in yogis, or during sleep. Enhanced vagal tone offers an explanation. The resting pacemaking activity decreases with age, however, because of the higher sympathetic tone this may not be observed in seniors – only the eventual tachycardia cannot reach the same level as in the young.

Negative chronotropic drugs (ß-blockers, Ca-channel blockers /verapamil and ditiazem/, digoxin, certain antiarrhythmic drugs) may induce sinus bradycardia. Other causes: vagus-stimulating reflexes (carotid massage, Valsalva manoeuver, ocular pressure, vomiting), sick sinus syndrome, inferior myocardial infarction, suppressed metabolic rate (hypothyroidism, hypothermia), obstructive jaundice, excitation of the vagus or central vagal nuclei (high intracranial pressure – Cushing reflex).

Fig. A1.27.

3. Sinus arrhythmia

Main feature is the changing R-R interval (Fig. A1.28.). The sinus node has both sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation, changes in the balance of the two tones causes sinus arrhythmia. Even normal sinus rhythm shows some instability: if the R-R intervals are absolutely regular, it refers to inability to influence the sinus pacemaker activity, i.e. it can be regarded as a sign of sinus injury. Respiratory arrhythmia is the most frequent form of sinus arrhythmia: during inspiration the heart rate increases, at expiration it decreases. Alternating excitation of pulmonary vagal receptors may be responsible for the phenomenon. Besides arrhythmia, the amplitudes of R waves are alternating. During inspiration the diaphragm moves downwards and the electric axis turns to left (the amplitude of R decreases in lead I and increases in lead III), during expiration the opposite changes can be observed.  Respiratory arrhythmia is more pronounced in the young, and may be absent in autonomic neuropathies (e.g. pronounced diabetes, elderlies).

Fig. A1.28.

4. Sick sinus syndrome (SSS)

Apart from old-age degenerative processes, SSS may be caused by several diseases like ischemic heart disease, carditis, amyloidosis, autoimmune diseases. SSS consists of various rhythm abnormalities (Fig. A1.29.). Atrial (ectopic) tachyarrhythmia forms are rather frequent, but bradycardic disorders also occur, or they appear in an alternating form (tachy-brady syndrome). In SSS severe sinus bradycardia, sino-auricular block, sinus arrest or chronic atrial fibrillation may also develop.

Fig. A1.29.

Sinus arrest: Sinus arrest may be evoked by SSS, myocardial infarction, myocarditis, excessive vagal tone, antiarrhythmic drugs. In case of sinus arrest the pacemaking must be taken over by a lower level pacemaker. If the arrest refers to one sinus stimulus only than an escape beat can be observed. In lasting absence of sinus activity, the developing escape rhythm saves life.

A1.2.1.4.2. Atrial rhythm disorders

1. Atrial extrasystole

Atrial extrasystole, the most frequent form of supraventricular extrasystole (SVES), is an early beat originating from an ectopic focus in the atria. Since the focus is away from the sinus node, the direction of atrial depolarization is altered, which results in an altered P wave morphology (in case the focus is near to the sinus node the P is positive, similar as a P of sinus origin, while stimuli from foci near the AV node produce negative P waves). Since the ventricular activation happens already via the normal conduction system, the morphology of QRS and the following repolarization are normal (or similar as in beats of sinus origin). Occasionally (if the coupling time of the ES is very short or the AV junction is still in the refractory state) the morphology of QRS may be altered.

Fig. A1.30.

Atrial ES of short coupling-time reaches the AV junction in absolute refractory period, the conduction is blocked and the ventricles are not activated – the abnormal P is not followed by QRS. If the AV node is already excitable, but the bundle braches are in relative refractory phase, the ventricular activation appears in the form of functional bundle branch block (usually the right bundle since this has a longer refractory period – see also functional bundle branch block, BBB).

Fig. A1.31.

All forms of SVES are usually subcompensated.

Atrial ES may occur in healthy persons in cases of nervousness, exhaustion, effect of alcohol, smoking or coffee, etc. It can be caused also by stretch of atrial wall (e.g. mitral stenosis, left ventricular failure), atrial dilation, pericarditis, myocarditis, pulmonary diseases, hyperthyroidism. It is clinically important if supraventricular reentry tachycardia is evoked. The blocked forms may be diagnostically misleading (if due to functional conduction disorder it appears as wide QRS).

2. Atrial tachycardia

This may develop by enhanced automatism of an atrial focus, or by reentry mechanism. The ectopic focus is in most cases in the right atrium. A ”warm up” phenomenon is often observed (the ectopic focus needs time for full activity), therefore the atrial frequency gradually increases. In atrial tachycardia the P morphology differs from P of sinus origin, its rate may be 100-250/min. In lower rates P can be conducted without block, in rates of >180-200 P/min the conduction is blocked (usually less reaches the ventricles, they may also be blocked within the ventricle).

Paroxysmal atrial tachycardia: In most cases it occurs following open heart surgery and usually ceases spontaneously.

Multifocal atrial tachycardia: more atrial foci produce stimuli, and therefore the morphology of P waves is variable. It is often seen in obstructive pulmonary diseases.

Permanent ectopic tachycardia (120-140/min): it occurs mostly in children and young persons – it may lead to heart failure.

Slow atrial tachycardia (80-140/min) a benign disorder, found mainly in the elderly.

3. Atrial fibrillation

This is the most common form of supraventricular tachyarrhythmia. Evoking factors may be elevation of atrial pressure (e.g. disorders of mitral valve or semilunar valve, hypertension, left ventricular failure), atrial ischemia (coronary heart disease), hyperthyroidism, SSS, pericarditis, pulmonary diseases with alveolar hypoxia, alcohol abuse, etc., but in some cases it is idiopathic (lone atrial fibrillation). In the elderly an uneven spread of the stimulus due to the fiber degeneration may contribute to fibrillation.

In atrial fibrillation pacemaking is totally chaotic, irregular. It develops mostly by reentry mechanism. The atrial fibers contract with an extremely high frequency (350-600/min), but ineffectively, since these contractions are incoordinated. There is no hemodynamically effective atrial contraction. The turbulent flow (or ’standing waves’) promotes atrial thrombus formation. To prevent brain embolism, the patient must have anticoagulant treatment. In the ECG the P waves are replaced by f-waves (particularly in V1) with continuously changing shape and amplitude. Occasionally, the very fine waves cannot change the ECG baseline. Impulses are transmitted in irregular intervals to the ventricles, therefore the ventricular contractions also appear irregularly, although the QRS complexes may remain normal (absolute arrhythmia or irregular irregularity), the R-R intervals are different.

The ventricular frequency – which may be about normal, bradyarrhythmic, tachyarrhythmic – changes with the actual conduction capacity of the AV junction. If over 200/min impulses are conducted, presence of accessory fibers must be assumed. If the ventricular rhythm becomes regular (usually bradycardic) AV dissociation is likely. Due to uneven ventricular filling and irregular contractions the peripheral pulse is irregular and unequal, pulse deficit may be observed. In case of tachyarrhythmia the diagnosis may be difficult (differences of the R-R intervals are not striking).

According to its course, paroxysmal, persisting and permanent forms of atrial fibrillation can be differentiated.

Fig. A1.32.

Fig. A1.33.

4. Atrial flutter

Less frequent than atrial fibrillation. It never occurs without organic heart disorder. Its causes are mainly the same as those of atrial fibrillation. It develops by reentry mechanism.

Regular atrial and usually regular ventricular activity is characteristic. The atrial frequency varies between 250-400/min. The ECG presents saw-tooth like F-waves. The AV conduction may vary (usually 2:1, 4:1), but in most cases regular (Fig. A1.34a.). However, the block ratio may change and suddenly great changes in heart rate may occur. Accordingly, this rhythm disorder is more unstable and its hemodynamic consequences are more problematic than those of atrial fibrillation. The ST segment and the T wave usually cannot be analyzed because of the F-waves. Although there is atrial activity, and coordinated contraction (this is also suggested by the retrograde venous pulse), the efficacy of the atrial contraction is less than normal, which makes high the danger of thrombus formation. Regular atrial and usually regular ventricular activity is characteristic (but arrhythmia absoluta may occasionally develop: Fig. A1.34b.).

A1.2.1.4.3. Junctional rhythm disorders

1. Junctional ES, escape beat, escape rhythm, accelerated junctional rhythm

Stimuli produced in the junction (a part of the AV node and the bundle of His) move towards the atria and the ventricles. The atria are depolarized backwards and the P wave is negative in II, III and aVF. Depending on whether the atria or the ventricles are reached first by the stimulus, the P wave may precede the QRS, may follow it, or may coincide with it. Since the ventricles are reached by the normal way, the QRS is normally thin and the repolarization (ST and T) is also normal.

The AV junctional ES (Fig. A1.35.) is rare form of SVES, its causes and significance are the same as those of the atrial ES.

Fig. A1.35.

Escape beat or escape rhythm usually develops because of absence of sinus effects. Continuous junctional rhythm may be expected in case of IIIrd degree SA-block, sinus arrest or IIIrd degree AV-block (supra-His, see AV-blocks). Lack of development of junctional escape rhythm suggests presence of disorder of AV-node or combined disorder of AV-node and sinus-node or IIIrd degree AV-block (infra-His).

In accelerated junctional rhythm the junctional frequency is between 60-100/min.

Fig. A1.36.

2. AV nodal reentry tachycardia (AVNRT)

AVNRT is characterized by sudden onset and similarly abrupt ending. It is based on functional microreentry, because some conducting fibers of the AV node have dissimilar conduction speed and refractory period. (fast conduction + long refractory period fibers mixed with others of slow conduction + short refractory period). In case of normal sinus rhythm, the stimulus is conducted through both fibers.

It may cause tachycardia by two ways: ’fast-slow’ or ’slow-fast’ AVNRT forms. The ’slow-fast’ form is more frequent. It is set on by an atrial ES: it finds the fast conducting fibers in refractory period after the last sinus stimulus, but the ES impulse is transmitted through the slow fibers (long PQ interval). In the meantime, the refractory state is over in the other fiber, allowing retrograde conduction from the ventricle and thereby closing the reentry circle. A regular paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia develops, in which the atria and the ventricles are activated practically simultaneously. Narrow QRS of normal morphology is characteristic, all impulses are transmitted, the frequency is generally 150-200/min. The retrograde P wave in leads II, III, aVF is either superimposed in the QRS, or deforms its terminal part.

In the other – rather rare, ’fast-slow’ – form of AVNRT a VES sets on a reentry cycle of opposite direction than in the previous form: the stimulus is transmitted through the fast fibers and returns from the ventricles by the slow fibers. The retrograde P wave is superimposed later, on or after the T wave.

The AVNRT is relatively more common in young, healthy persons, who tolerate it quite well. Since it is of supraventricular origin, heart rate can be influenced/normalized by vagal stimulation (e.g. Valsalva manoeuver, carotid massage) or administration of adenosine.

Fig. A1.37.

12. AV nodal reciprocal tachycardia (AV reentry tachycardia involving the accessory fiber: AVRT)

AVRT is a macroreentry, affecting both the AV node and an accessory fiber. These congenital fibers (see preexcitation syndromes, ch. A1.2.2.4.) offer a faster conduction pathway besides the physiological conduction system, thereby a part of the myocardium is activated earlier than the physiological conducting system, therefor a part of the myocardium is activated earlier than the physiological activation.

The developing reentry tachycardia is orthodromic AVRT, if the stimulus is conducted in anterograde direction through the AV-node, but it is conducted back retrogradely through the accessory fiber (the QRS is narrow, due to anterograde activation of the ventricles). In case of antidromic AVRT the stimulus circles in the other direction, therefore a broad-QRS tachycardia develops. Clinically, most important of these abnormalities is the Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome.

The more frequent form of WPW syndrome is the orthodromic AV reciprocal tachycardia. The stimulus activates the ventricles by the normal route, then it returns to the atria through the accessory fiber, then again to the ventricle through the AV-node. It is usually initiated by an ES. There is no preexcitation, no delta wave, the QRS is narrow, and the retrograde atrial activation gives negative P waves in II-III-aVF.

In case of antidromic AV reciprocal tachycardia the ventricles are activated through the accessory fiber (the QRS preexcited, bizarre). The stimulus returns through the AV-node to close the reentry cycle. Due to the wide QRS complexes it is difficult to differentiate from ventricular tachycardia.

A1.2.1.5. VENTRICULAR RHYTHM DISORDERS

1. Ventricular extrasystole (VES)

It is an early beat (as compared with the basic rhythm) originating from a ventricular focus. It may be caused by reentry at the Purkinje fibers, enhanced automatism, or triggered activation.

Its basic features are that it is premature, it is not preceded by P wave, the QRS is broad, (>0.12 s) and has a bizarre shape since the ventricles are activated not through the normal conducting system, but by muscle conduction. The abnormal depolarization is accompanied by secondary repolarization (ST, T) disorders. The VES is usually followed by complete compensatory pause (Fig. A1.38.).

Fig. A1.38.

A life-threatening form is the so-called R-on-T phenomenon (VES falls on the preceding T-wave). The danger is that it may provoke ventricular fibrillation, since in this vulnerable phase some fibers can already be activated, the others are still in refractory period. The latter fibers will be activated one-by-one as their refractory period is over – the process goes on: muscle conduction activates again the fibers of the first group.

A single VES may occur on healthy persons (it usually originates from the right ventricle), but more often in ischemic heart disease, acute myocardial infarction, dilative cardiomyopathy, digitalis intoxication, hypoxia, acidosis, application of sympathomimetic drugs.

2. Ventricular parasystolia

Besides the sinus pacemaker, a ventricular pacemaking focus also functions regularly and independently of its environment, since unidirectional block that surrounds it does not allow sinus impulses to reach this point. The refractory state of the ventricle will determine which of the two pacemakers will actually activate the ventricles. Characteristically, the coupling-times of parasystolic beats are changing. The monomorphic broad parasystolic QRS-complexes follow each other with certain regularity, e.g. every 12th beat is parasystolic QRS.

3. Accelerated ventricular rhythm

Accelerated idioventricular rhythm means an unusually high ventricular frequency, which is higher than the ventricular escape rhythm, but usually does not exceed 100/min. It is most often observed in the acute phase of myocardial infarction.

4. Ventricular tachycardia

Often has reentry in the background, induced by ischemic heart disease. It is always pathological. Usually VES (sometimes supraventricular tachycardia) is the initiating factor. Its main features are: Broad QRS complexes, regular rhythm with 100-250/min frequency – fusion beat and captured beat may occur. It may be transformed into ventricular fibrillation.

It develops mostly in organic heart diseases (coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, aortic stenosis, congestive and hypertrophic cardiomyopathies, prolapse of the mitral valve, etc.), but it may also appear in hypokalemia, long Q-T syndrome, or as a cardiac manifestation of drugs, tumors, sarcoidosis, amyloidosis, scleroderma.

Fig. A1.40.

Classification of forms of ventricular tachycardia:

Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia: minimum 3 consecutive, maximum 30 s duration, spontaneously ceasing ectopic ventricular beats, without significant hemodynamic consequences.  A sustained ventricular tachycardia persists longer than 30 s and causes significant hemodynamic consequences – this must be stopped immediately. According to QRS morphology monomorphic, polymorphic, bidirectional and torsade de pointes tachycardia may occur. According to the frequency sloe, fast forms and ventricular flutter (>200/min) or ventricular fibrillation (>300/min) can be differentiated. According to coupling-time: R-on-T phenomenon (very short switch-time, unfavorable prognosis).

Chaotic ventricular tachycardia (torsade de pointes): the QRS axis continuously changes, therefore continuously periodically changing QRS (spindle-like) morphology is observed.

Repetitive, monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (Gallavardin-type): idiopathic ventricular tachycardia, the 5-10 contraction episodes of non-sustained tachycardia are interrupted by 1-1 sinus beat.

Bidirectional tachycardia: it is induced by an impulse from the supra-bifurcational region of the left bundle branch, which is conducted once by the anterior, then by the posterior fibers. In the ECG, besides a right bundle branch block, alternating left anterior and left posterior hemiblock is shown.

Fig. A1.41.

Ventricular flutter: the 200-300/min frequency ventricular tachycardia shows a characteristic sinusoid ECG curve. The small pulse-waves often cannot be sensed (pulseless tachycardia).

Fig. A1.42.

It may cause a diagnostic problem that a supraventricular tachycardia (with increasing frequency) may appear as wide-QRS tachycardia. Differentiation is helped by the finding that the rate of a supraventricular tachycardia (but not ventricular tachycardia) decreases for carotid massage. Besides, extremely wide QRS (>0.16 s) suggests ventricular pacemaking, just as a sign of AV dissociation, presence of fusion beat or captured beat.

Fig. A1.43.

5. Ventricular fibrillation

Impulses exceeding 300/min cause totally uncoordinated high-frequency fiber contractions, which do not result in effective ventricular contraction. In most cases it develops from ventricular tachycardia as a terminal disorder of rhythm. Its symptoms are similar as in Stokes-Adams syndrome. Without reanimation it is lethal.

Causes: AMI, severe ischemic heart disease, ischemic cardiomyopathy, electrical shock, lightning, hypokalemia, hyperkalemia, hypothermia, heat stroke, trauma, drugs (chloroform, sympathomimetics), extreme pH abnormalities, acute pancreatitis.

Fig. A1.44.

A1.2.2. DISORDERS OF CONDUCTION

Damages of the physiological conduction system cause conduction disorders, which, according to the location of the disorder may be sinoatrial (SA), atrioventricular (AV) or intraventricular blocks. According to severity, the blocks can be categorized as Ist, IInd, IIIrd degree blocks (Ist degree block only means slow conduction, but all impulse is transmitted, in the IInd degree blocks some stimuli are conducted, some others are not, in IIIrd degree block there is no conduction through the given point). Another disorder of conduction is the too fast conduction (see pre-excitation syndromes).

A1.2.2.1. SINOATRIAL (SA) BLOCK

1. First degree SA-block

In Ist degree SA block the stimulus spreads slowly from the sinus node to the atria. Since the activity of the sinus node itself cannot be recorded by surface ECG (only by intracardiac ECG), the prolonged conduction of Ist degree SA block cannot be noticed by routine ECG recording.

2. Second degree SA-block

Two types are known: In type-I the conduction time is getting longer from beat to beat, then a stimulus is not transmitted to the atria. In the ECG the P-P intervals gradually decrease, which is followed by a long P-P interval sine one P wave (adjoining QRS) is missing. Afterwards the cycle starts again (for detailed explanation se: second degree AV block).

In type-II normal SA conductions are suddenly followed by one omission, one beat (both P and QRS waves) is completely missing. The R-R interval is twice normal.

Fig. A1.45.

3. Third degree SA-block

In IIIrd degree SA block the sinus stimulus is never conducted to the atria. The ECG corresponds to the picture of sinus arrest, i.e. isoelectric line is observed, as long as a lower-level pacemaker switches on and produces an escape rhythm. Without this the Stokes–Adams syndrome is to be expected. By surface ECG, a third degree SA block cannot be differentiated from sinus arrest.

A1.2.2.2. ATRIOVENTRICULAR (AV) BLOCK

1. First degree AV-block

All stimuli are conducted from the atria to the ventricles (all P waves are followed by QRS), only slowly. This leads to extension of the PQ interval (>0.20 s). Although appears unimportant, it may cover severe disorders, easily changes for IIIrd degree AV block (!).

Possible causes: AMI (inferior), high vagal tone, ischemic heart disease, cardiomyopathies, senile degenerative processes, acute rheumatic carditis, endocarditis, drugs (e.g. digitalis, β-blockers, verapamil), hypokalemia, diphtheria, Lyme-disease.

Fig. A1.46.

2. Second degree AV-block

Not all P waves are followed by QRS complex, but the blocked P wave is followed lately by a conducted P wave. At a time, the conduction of only one supraventricular impulse is omitted.

Fig. A1.47.

2/a. Mobitz I type second degree AV block (Wenckebach-periodicity):

The PQ interval is gradually extended, i.e. the conduction is becoming more and more difficult (the intervals between QRS complexes decreases), until one impulse is not conducted at all. In ECG the P wave is not followed by QRS. (The presence of P wave differentiates from second degree SA block). The block ratio shows how many P waves are transmitted out of a certain number (e.g. 3:3 = 2 transmitted out of 3).  In Mobitz type-I block the His-bundle ECG shows a damage above the His-bundle (supra-His). This induces junctional escape rhythm, if progresses to IIIrd degree AV block (rarely). (Wenckebach point is the frequency at which Mobitz type-I AV block develops. This is normally a very high heart rate: 220-age).

Causes: AMI (inferior), mostly disease of the right coronary (this supplies arteries for the AV node), digitalis intoxication, myocarditis, degenerative heart diseases.

Fig. A1.48.

2/b. Mobitz II type second degree AV block:

All of a sudden, one P wave is not followed by QRS and T, but in the transmitted beats the PQ interval is standard (may be normal or prolonged). Since the ventricular rate is low, appearance of escape beats is common. It often progresses into complete AV block. In this case the block develops due to damage of the distal part of the bundle of His (infra-His) and only ventricular escape rhythm is possible.

Causes: AMI (anterior), cardiomyopathies.

Fig. A1.49.

High-grade AV-block means that not one, but two or more consecutive P waves are not followed by QRS, i.e. conduction of more impulses is blocked, e.g. 3:1 or 4:1 blocks. It differs from IIIrd degree AV block that all QRS are normal and all of them are preceded by a P wave.

Fig. A1.50.

3. Third degree (complete) AV-block:

In IIIrd degree AV block no supraventricular impulses can be conducted to the ventricles. Pacemaking is taken over by a point of lower level of automatism and, depending on the level of the block, junctional (40-60/min) or ventricular (20-40/min) escape rhythm develops, with low frequency. Escape rhythms are coupled with AV dissociation, i.e. the atria and the ventricles are activated independently. In the ECG the P waves and QRS complexes are independent, and they appear in their own frequencies. In case of missing escape rhythm or if it is initiated too late, Stokes-Adams syndrome may be observed.

Causes: myocarditis, infiltrative/degenerative processes of the AV node, ischemic heart disease, AMI, iatrogenic (e.g. digitalis intoxication), or may be congenital.

Fig. A1.51.

A1.2.2.3. INTRAVENTRICULAR CONDUCTION DISORDERS

If conduction is blocked through one bundle branch (BB), the corresponding ventricle will be activated from the normally activated ventricle by myocardial fibers, slowly. The slow spread of impulse leads to widening and deformation of QRS. Damage of BB-s or their fascicles usually indicates local ischemia/degeneration.

In complete bundle branch block (BBB) the QRS duration may reach or exceed 120 ms. The blocked side is activated by slow muscle conduction from the side of intact bundle branch – simultaneously a striking change in QRS axis can be observed in QRS morphology: first the axis points for a short time towards the intact side, then towards the blocked side (this widens the QRS complex).

In incomplete BBB the QRS duration is 100-120 ms, otherwise the ECG changes are similar as in complete block.

Hemiblock or fascicular block occurs if the anterior and posterior fascicle of the left bundle branch (LBB) is damaged. Due to interfascicular connections the activation time of the ventricles (VAT) does not change too much, but the direction of impulse spreading (axis) is strongly altered. In the ECG narrow QRS and extreme axis deviations are characteristic: left anterior hemiblock leads to extreme left, posterior hemiblock to extreme right axis deviation.

Frequency-dependent (functional) BBB is usually caused by dissimilar refractory periods in the two bundle branches. With increasing heart rate, mainly the right bundle branch (RBB) is blocked, because of it longer refractory period. SVES may also show the picture of functional RBBB – occasionally a supraventricular tachycardia with wide QRS complexes may become similar as a ventricular tachycardia (for differentiation: carotid massage).

Intermittant BBB: normal and BBB (wide) QRS complexes alternate.

Alternating BBB: LBBB and RBBB alternate from beat-to-beat. Cause: usually tachycardia.

1. Right bundle branch block (RBBB)

In RBBB the depolarization of the septum is normal, since the septum is supplied by the left branch. The early part of QRS remains normal (therefore signs of an eventual AMI can be shown). After septal activation the left ventricular regions are normally activated, then the right ventricle will be activated slowly through conduction by muscle fibers. Due to slow depolarization the QRS becomes wide (>0.12 s = 120 ms). During activation of the right ventricle the vector points forward and to the right, in leads representing the left ventricle (I, II, aVL, V5-6), wide, deep S wave is seen, while in right-side chest leads (V1-2) wide and split R and R’ waves (rsR’) may be observed. In the ECG the long VAT (>40 ms) has a diagnostic value. The ST and T cannot be evaluated, and they show secondary repolarization abnormalities.

Fig. A1.52.

In classic forms the initial part of BBB is damaged, while in Wilson-block rather the lower part of BBB. Due to common blood supply, it is often coupled with anterior hemiblock (Lenègre-syndrome).

The RBB is thinner, more vulnerable and it is damaged more easily and more often than the LBB. Occasionally RBBB may occur in healthy persons. It may be present transiently in pulmonary embolism. The most frequent pathological causes of RBBB are atrial septum defect, right ventricular strain, carditis, coronary disease, degenerative heart diseases.

2. Left bundle branch block (LBBB)

After a short common part, the LBB divides into an anterior and posterior fascicle. The anterior fascicle runs subendocardially at the upper anterior part of the left ventricle, the posterior one runs in lower posterior position at the diaphragmatic part of the left ventricle.   In complete LBBB both the septum and the left ventricle are activated from the right ventricle. The long VAT causes extension of QRS. Since the septal activation is abnormal, the whole QRS vector differs from the normal and the whole QRS wave is abnormal. Such QRS is followed by secondary disorder of repolarization.

Features of LBBBi: the QRS duration is > 0.12 s, in leads representing the left ventricle (I, aVL, V5-6) Q is missing (activation disorder of the septum), the R wave is uneven and split. The prolonged VAT in V5-6 (>80 ms) is of diagnostic value. In V1-2 the dominant part of depolarization is negative (because the left ventricle is activated from the right one), QS, or rS patterns are characteristic. Since the initial QRS vector is affected, earlier infections cannot be evaluated.

LBBB occurs always in heart disease: mostly in ischemic heart disease, in severe left ventricular hypertrophy, since it runs superficially underneath the endocardium.

Fig. A1.53.

Fig. A1.54.

3. Hemiblocks

3/a Left anterior hemiblock: In left anterior hemiblock, the posterobasal part of the ventricle is activated earlier than the anterior part. Therefore, the early activation points downwards, to the right and slightly forwards. In I, aVL q, in II, III, aVF r wave is to be expected. Later the vector turns to the left and upwards, resulting in extreme left deviation, (more negative than the -30⁰) and in appearance the qR complex in I, aVL and rS in II, III, aVF. The QRS duration does not exceed 120 ms (80-120 ms). In chest leads there is no diagnostically important depolarization disorder (except that the normal propagation of R wave is absent).

Fig. A1.55.

3/b Left posterior hemiblock: In left posterior hemiblock, first the upper anterior part of the ventricle is activated, then the vector turns downwards and to the right, following the direction of flow of the impulse.  Extreme right deviation develops (>+110⁰), rS is seen in leads I, aVL, qR in II, III, aVF and QRS does not exceed 120 ms. If the axis was shifted otherwise to the left, (e.g. left ventricular hypertrophy), it is not sure that the fascicular block can induce an extreme axis deviation to the right, but as compared with the ECG prior to the development of the block, the shift of the axis may well be demonstrated. This occurs rather rarely.

Fig. A1.56.

A1.2.2.4. PRE-EXCITATION SYNDROMES

In pre-excitation syndromes an accessory conducting fiber, surrounding the normal conduction system, causes early activation of the ventricles.

1. Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome (WPW)

A congenital abnormality, with an accessory atrio-ventricular conducting fiber (Kent fiber), which affects about 1% of the population.  During sinus rhythm, the atrial impulse reaches the ventricles through this fiber faster than through the AV node, and it sets on a ventricular depolarization. This explains that the PQ interval is <120 ms. This impulse spreads in the ventricular myocardium by muscle conduction, which gives an atypical ventricular depolarization in the ECG shape of a delta wave at the early part of the QRS complex (Fig. A1.57.). The stimulus is also conducted by a normal delay through the AV node, and it produces a physiological activation of the ventricles – this will finish the QRS at normal time. The QRS is widened (>120 ms), because it starts earlier but finishes at the normal time. In type-A of WPW syndrome the fiber is at the left ventricle, in type-B in the right one. The appearance of delta waves in various leads may allow conclusions regarding the type. The abnormal depolarization is followed by secondary repolarization abnormalities (ST and T disorders).

Besides AV nodal reciprocal tachycardia, other rhythm abnormalities are also more common in patients of WPW syndrome. In contrast to the AV node, the accessory fiber does not delay the conduction of the impulse thus, in cases of supraventricular tachycardia the ventricular rate may also exceed 200/min – this possibility arises in all cases of supraventricular tachycardia that cause ventricular rates above 200/min, since the maximal transmission rate through the AV node is 180-200/min.

Fig. A1.57.

Almost any form of atrial impulses can be transmitted through the fast-conducting short-refractory accessory fibers, therefore its combination with atrial fibrillation or flutter induce life-threatening rhythm abnormalities (ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation). Such rhythm abnormalities may be provoked by drugs suppressing the AV conduction (e.g. digitalis, verapamil).

Intermittant preexcitation: It is indicated by sudden disappearance of delta waves. It can develop on slowly conducting accessory fibers (the automatic tone may influence the refractory period of these fibers). The probability of dangerous ventricular disorders is small.

Latent preexcitation: at normal sinus rhythm there is no conduction through the accessory fiber, but in atrial tachycardia, atrial fibrillation through these fibers anterograde conduction starts.

2. Lown-Ganong-Levine syndrome (LGL)

In LGL syndrome the James fibers connect the atria and the bundle of His. The transmission becomes shorter, but the ventricular activation is completely normal (Fig. A1.58.). In the ECG short PQ interval and normal QRS can be observed. The patients have a tendency for tachycardia, the normal or pathological atrial impulses reach the ventricles without difficulty (the filter role of the AV node is missing). LGL is less frequent than the WPW syndrome.

Fig. A1.58.

A1.2.3. DISORDERS OF MYOCARDIAL OXYGEN SUPPLY

A1.2.3.1. GRADES OF MYOCARDIAL ISCHEMIA

1. Ischemia/hypoxia

At the beginning not the resting potential, only the action potential is influenced by the oxygen deficiency. The action potentials become shorter, what alters the T wave in the ECG. Subendocardial ischemia leads to high, pointed, positive T waves, in case of subepicardial-transmural ischemia pointed negative symmetrical T wave (”coronary T”) develops. The changes are reversible, and the waves are normalized with normalization of the oxygen supply.

2. Injury

With progression – mainly due to decreased function of Na+/K+ ATPase the IC level of K+ decreases and hypopolarization develops (the normal electropositivity of the injured fiber decreases, it is relatively electronegative to the intact fibers). This is taken as current of injury, since after completing repolarization there remains a potential-difference between the intact and injured areas (the injured areas are relatively electronegative). This difference shifts the baseline of the ECG, the direction and extent of which is various leads depends on the location and extension of the injury, and in the analysis it is regarded as a virtual shift of the ST segment.

Fig. A1.59.

a) Diffuse subendocardial injury: The dipole-vectors from the current of injury point from the subendocardial to the subepicardial regions with a summation vector of small size and the same direction as the R vector. In most leads this gives a positive deflection and the baseline is shifted upwards (although much less than the R wave). When the ventricles are fully depolarized, the potential difference between the injured and intact regions disappears, in the J point the baseline returns to its normal position: in most leads of the ECG a virtual ST depression is seen. This is how ST depression develops in stabile angina pectoris in all leads where the main deflection of the QRS is positive.

b) Diffuse subepicardial/transmural injury: The subendocardial intact areas are relatively positive to the injured subepicardial regions. The summation vector is again small, but points in the opposite direction as the R vector. The projection of the current of injury produces a negative deflection in most leads and causes a baseline shift downwards. At full depolarization, at the J point the normal baseline is reached, which appears elevated. In the ECG a virtual ST elevation (a concordant shift in the main deflection of the QRS complex) is observed in all leads (e.g. pericarditis).

Fig. A1.60.

c) Localized injury: By the same mechanism, in leads towards which the injury points, there is a virtual ST elevation, in the opposite leads a virtual depression (mirror image) develops.

Fig. A1.61.

If the coronary perfusion normalizes, these changes may be reversible, or in lasting impairment of coronary perfusion myocardial infarction develops.

3. Necrosis:

In the ECG a definite sign of myocardial necrosis is the pathological Q wave in extremity leads, or the QS complex in chest leads in case of transmural infarction (Fig. A1.62.). In the necrotized region the transmembrane potential disappears and these cells cannot be depolarized. This region is electronegative in comparison with the intact regions. The area of transmural necrosis behaves as an electrical window through which it can be observed what happens inside. The electrode above this region picks up signals from the opposite intact wall of the ventricle. In the intact wall the impulse moves away from this electrode and this results in a single negative deflection, the QS complex. If the necrosis is not transmural, but intact and necrotic areas are mixed, there is no QS, but a reduction of R wave (lack of normal R propagation in chest leads) can be observed (Fig. A1.63.), with or without pathological Q in extremity leads.

Fig. A1.62.

Pathological Q wave: In the extremity leads it reaches or exceeds 40 ms, its amplitude is at least 25% of the corresponding R wave. It may appear after a few hours of coronary occlusion. The size (deepness) of the wave indicates how severely the myocardium is affected in end-epicardial direction. False Q wave may appear in pulmonary embolism, left ventricular hypertrophy, WPW syndrome, and in cardiomyopathies.

Fig. A1.63.

A1.2.3.2. (ACUTE) MYOCARDIAL INFARCTION (AMI)

Myocardial infarction characteristically affects the left ventricle, very rarely the right one. From electrophysiological aspects, the main problem is the decrease in the IC concentration of K+ and the consequent hypopolarization of cells. This phenomenon starts at the ischemic outer edges of the infarction and progresses inwards through a zone of injury to the central necrosis of the infarction. In the ECG with the gradual completion of the symptoms of infarction first the T wave changes and ”moving” ST, then a large Q and decreasing R wave will be characteristic. In the progression of the infarction there are hyperacute – acute – chronic phases.

1. Hyperacute phase:

A pointed positive T wave may be the first sign of subendocardial ischemia. Within minutes after the coronary occlusion a classic, dome-shaped, upward convex ST-elevation (>0.1 mV) develops in at least two of the leads representing the region of the injured myocardium. In this phase the T is still high, positive, pointed (”T en dome”). In the opposite leads ST depression is seen as a mirror-image, e.g. in inferior ST elevation is seen in the region representing the inferior wall (e.g.   aVF), while in leads of the anterior region ST depression is observed. The hyperacute phase lasts for a few hours only. The same picture is seen in Prinzmetal angina, but this is transient, after the attack the ECG is normalized.

2. Acute phase:

A few hours after the occlusion symptoms of necrosis start showing: Q wave increases (occasionally Q wave may not increase, this is named non-Q infarction), and the reduction of R wave starts in this phase. During the acute phase ST elevation gradually becomes smaller and the T wave turns negative.

From the location of ST elevations and Q wave conclusions can be drawn regarding the localization of the infarction:

1. Septal: V1-V2

2. Anteroseptal: V1-V4

3. Anterior: V2-V4

4. Extensive anterior: I-aVL-V1-V6

5. Anterolateral: I-aVL-V6

6. Apical: V5-V6

7. Inferior: II-III-aVF

8. Posterior: ’mirror-image’ changes: V1-3  (VD1-3, V7-9,)

9. Right ventricle: V1-V2  (V4R-V5R)

Fig. A1.64.

Posterior myocardial infarction (Fig. A.1.65.): In V1-3 (typically in V2) of the routine 12-lead ECG large R wave, ST depression and positive T wave are seen (mirroring the typical appearance of infarction). The typical infarction morphology could be demonstrated in dorsal leads (which are not used routinely). Sometimes it is named as infero-basal infarction.

Right ventricular infarction is suggested by ST elevation in V1 and it might be verified by the special leads on the right chest wall (V3R-V4R): here ST elevation and Q wave are present. Lone right ventricular infarction is a rarity, but it may join to postero-inferior infarctions.

Fig. A1.65.

Fig. A1.66.

3. Chronic phase:

Fig. A1.67.

Reconstruction of the necrotic parts may take several months, the final ECG curve (which is not uniform) is established at this time. In the extremity leads showing the infarction the pathological Q and the R reduction (in anterior wall necrosis also the QS complex) are sustained, but the ST is normalized (sustained ST elevation refers to ventricular aneurysm) and the T wave is usually normalized (after an extensive infarction it may remain flat or negative).

In the clinical practice myocardial infarctions without ST elevation (NSTEMI) are more frequent than tose with typical ST elevation (STEMI). Diagnosis is possible not by the ECG, but by the clinical symptoms, enzyme levels, or echocardiography. Even in these cases, ECG exhibits deviations from normal: mainly ST depression and T inversion are seen.

Fig. A1.68.

A1.2.3.3. ANGINA PECTORIS

1. Effort angina pectoris (stabile angina)

Due to narrowing of the coronary vessels and short-term reversible ischemia of the myocardium, certain loads (e.g. a certain grade of exercise) may evoke deep, pressing, constrictive type (not sharp!) of chest pain.

Fig. A1.69.

Since blood is supplied to the myocardium from the subepicardial zone to the subendocardial one, exercise with narrow coronaries will cause disturbance of blood supply first in the subendocardial region. Besides the clinical symptoms of angina pectoris, subendothelial injury induces transient ST depression (horizontal, or descending forms) above the affected regions (rather widespread) or during exercise at least in V4-V6.

2. Prinzmetal angina

It appears during rest, without exercise, as a consequence of spasm of bigger epicardial coronary artery. According to the symptoms of subepicardial/transmural injuries, it is characterized by ST elevation, although it is transient. Both ECG and the clinical symptoms resemble the hyperacute phase of AMI, but in this case the ECG is normalized when the attack is over.

Fig. A1.70.

A1.2.3.4. PERICARDITIS

In acute (diffuse) pericarditis the ST-level is elevated in all leads (with concordant main deflection) (cf. Fig. A1.60.). In prolonged processes the ST normalizes, the T wave becomes inverted.

Fig. A1.71.

A1.2.4. ATRIAL DILATATION, VENTRICULAR HYPERTROPHY

A1.2.4.1. ATRIAL DILATATION

Due to the thin wall of the atria, upon increased pressure or volume they dilate, they can hardly hypertrophize. The ECG signs of atrial dilation are not sensitive, not specific. In case of suspicion of atrial dilation by the ECG, it can be verified by echocardiography.

Enlargement of the right atrium

Activation of the right atrium is responsible for the first part of P wave. Right atrial enlargement is suggested by pulmonary P: in leads II, III, aVF pointed, large (>0.25 mV), but not broad P wave. In V1 the first part of the biphasic P is bigger.

Right atrial enlargement may be caused by chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic respiratory failure, chronic cor pulmonale, left-to-right shunt, congenital or acquired abnormalities of pulmonary and tricuspid valves, or right ventricular hypertrophy.

Fig. A1.72.

2. Enlargement of the left atrium

The second part of P wave indicates activation of the left atrium. The main sign of left atrial enlargement is the mitral P: broad (>0.1 s) split P wave in I, II, V5-6. In V1-2 the so-called P-terminal force is seen: the terminal negative component of the biphasic P wave is enlarged (>0.04 s, depth >0.1 mV, area underneath the isoelectric line >1mm2).

It is often caused by mitral insufficiency, left ventricular hypertrophy, hypertension, cardiomyopathies, aortic valve insufficiency, right-to-left shunt.

Fig. A1.73.

3. Biatrial enlargement

P-biatriale: in II, III, aVF the P wave is broad and high (>0.25 mV and >0.10 s), and in V1-2 both components of the biphasic P wave exceed 0.1 mV and the terminal negative component is longer than 0.04 s.

It is caused by combination of the factors affecting the two atria.

Enlargement of one or both atria predisposes to atrial fibrillation.

A1.2.4.2. VENTRICULAR HYPERTROPHY

Acute ventricular load (e.g. pulmonary embolism) induces transient ECG disorders. Lasting pressure or volume loads induce hypertrophy of the ventricular wall, which causes persisting ECG abnormalities. The large muscle mass shifts the electrical axis and leads to changes in QRS. The axis is directed towards the hypertrophized ventricle. Due to the large muscle mass the depolarization is extended, the VAT is long, the QRS is slightly (max. 0.12 s) widened. Depolarization of the great muscle mass results in high voltage at the hypertrophized ventricles. Since capillarization does not keep pace with the increasing mass, relative hypoperfusion and ischemia may develop, causing repolarization (ST and T) abnormalities. Stretch of the ventricular wall (strain) is also presumed to contribute to these changes.

N.B.: in contrast to hypertrophy, in cases of dislocation the axis deviation is not accompanied by any pathological changes, the morphologies of all waves are normal (the axes of all waves are shifted parallel, in the same direction and the same degree). In dislocation the position of the heart is changed upon some external influence: e.g. dislocation to the left may be caused by elevation of the diaphragm in obesity, ascites, pregnancy, abdominal tumor, etc., but the intact heart may be pushed or pulled by intrathoracic factors as well.

1. Left ventricular hypertrophy

Due to the enlarged mas of the left ventricle the electrical axis turns to the left and backwards, resulting in left-type (sometimes horizontal) axis. The large muscle mass also increases the potentials, and in the leads representing the left ventricle (I, aVL, V5-6) high R waves, in those representing the right ventricle (V1-3) deep S waves are observed. The Sokolov index (V5R+V1S >35 mm) suggests hypertrophy. In left ventricular hypertrophy the transient zone is shifted to the left (V5-6). Depolarizing the great muscle mass takes longer time, therefore the VAT is >45 ms in the leads showing the left ventricle (V5-6).

The high wall tension and the relative ischemia may lead to descending type of ST depression and negative T wave (signs of strain) in leads representing the left ventricle.

Most frequent evoking factors: hypertension, aortic stenosis.

Fig. A1.74.

2. Right ventricular hypertrophy

The QRS vector points to the right, forwards and downwards, resulting in right-type axis deviation. In leads representing the right ventricle (V1-2) enhanced R wave (>0.7 mV), in those representing the left ventricles (V5-6) deep S waves are seen. In the right ventricular leads (V1-2) the VAT is >40 ms. In the same leads descending ST depression and T wave inversion may occur (signs of strain).

Fig. A1.75.

Acute load of the right ventricle is accompanied by transient ECG disorders. Mostly pulmonary embolism or pneumothorax is the evoking factor. Several disorders may appear in the ECG, like a combination of S wave in I, Q wave in II and negative T waves, or in other cases acute pulmonary P, in leads II, III, aVF descending ST depression with inverted T waves, but a sinus tachycardia or paroxysmal atrial arrhythmia at vertical or right axis may also be characteristic.

A1.2.5. ELECTROLYTE DISORDERS

A1.2.5.1. POTASSIUM

Abnormalities of se-K level may cause life-threatening disorders of rhythm.

1. Hypokalemia

In hypokalemia the cell membrane is hyperpolarized, i.e. the resting potential is high it is more difficult to reach the threshold potential, but the activity of ectopic foci that can produce stimuli is enhanced.

The ECG signs: prominent U waves, flat T waves, eventually T inversion, the QT interval is usually at the upper end of the normal, increased probability of SVES, VES, in severe cases vetricular tachycardia (torsade de pointes), ventricular fibrillation may develop.

Fig. A1.76.

2. Hyperkalemia

In hyperkalemia the resting potential of the cells decreases and this leads to poor conduction.

a) mild hyperkalemia: High, pointed T waves, with usually narrow base (at higher concentrations the base becomes broader), the R amplitude decreases.

b) exceeding 6,5 mmol/l: PQ is prolonged, the P wave decreases, it may disappear (>8.5 mmol/l), the QRS becomes wider, the ST is often depressed, in severe cases slow idioventricular rhythm, or ventricular fibrillation (usually at >8.5 mmol/l) may develop.

Fig. A1.77.

A1.2.5.2. CALCIUM

1. Hypocalcemia

The QT interval is prolonged due to isolated extension of the ST segment. Inversion of T wave is possible.

Fig. A1.78.

2. Hypercalcemia:

The QT interval is shorter, the T wave starts soon after the QRS complex, the T wave is broader and rounded.

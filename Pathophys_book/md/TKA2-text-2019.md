A2. EXAMINATION OF RESPIRATORY FUNCTIONS

Tests of respiratory functions provide information about movement of gases in and out of the lungs via the conducting zone and about alveolo-capillary gas exchange, i.e. the diffusion.

These tests help in the differential diagnostics of dyspnea, permit differentiation of restrictive and obstructive respiratory disorders, and these tests are important in preoperative assessment of operational risk. Besides, these tests are used to measure the effect of bronchodilating medications and can be used to follow the functional state of the patient. They are also useful in screenings, and in recognizing any deterioration of respiratory parameters in time, e.g. the obstructive respiratory disorders of smokers can be detected at an early stage.

A2.1. ANALYSIS OF VENTILATION

Earlier, ventilation was studied by circuit water seal spirometers: the patient breathed in and out of a bell-spirometer, the volumes were calculated from the movements of the bell. Nowadays automated-flow spirometers are widely used, which automatically calculate expiratory and inspiratory volumes, flow rate and present the volume/flow curve. In special cases further parameters can be determined by body-plethysmographs (e.g. airway resistance, intrathoracic gas-volume, total lung capacity).

Interpretation of lung function tests include the analysis of static and dynamic parameters, volume/flow curves, and indices of respiratory mechanics are also estimated. The normal value of these parameters depends on gender, age, body height (in some cases also on body weight). Therefore, prior to the examination the normal (‘expected’) value should be calculated for each patient (now this is done by computer).  The measured values are to be compared with this, and expressed both in absolute terms and in % of the ‘expected’ value. The ‘expected’ value of FEV1, for example, decreases by 20 ml/year, thus the normal value is greater in young than in old persons. Evaluations are easier if the measured data are expressed as % of the ‘expected’ value.

A.2.1.1. PARAMETERS OF VENTILATION

A.2.1.1.1. STATIC PARAMETERS

Static parameters are independent of time factor – the speed of respiration is adjusted to the measure of the maneuver. They change mainly in restrictive respiratory disorders. During the test, the patient first breathes quietly, then slowly inspires as much as possible, finally expires all what can be blown out (Fig. A2.1.).

I. STATIC LUNG VOLUMES:

Tidal volume (VT): the amount of inspired air with a quiet breath (500-600 ml).

Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV): the maximal amount of air that can still be inspired following a quiet breath (ca. 2500 ml).

Expiratory reserve volume (ERV): the maximal amount of air that can be blown out following a quiet expiration (ca. 1000 ml). Together with IRV, this serves respiratory adaptation in various situations (e.g. fever, exercise).

Residual volume (RV): the amount of air remaining in the lung after maximal expiration. It cannot be emptied. It has mechanical and chemical buffer roles, i.e. despite cyclic breathing it secures continuity of gas composition and exchange, also prevents the collapse of the alveoli.

Fig. A2.1.

II. STATIC LUNG CAPACITIES:

Total lung capacity (TLC(VC+RV): The air content of the lung following maximal inspiration.

Vitalcapacity (VC(IRV+VT+ERV): The air content between the endpoints of maximal inspiration and maximal expiration. It may be measured in two fractions: Inspiratory VC = maximal slow inspiration after a normal expiration, and Expiratory VC = maximal slow expiration after a normal inspiration.

Inspiratory capacity IC(VT+IRV): The amount of air that can be inspired after a normal expiration.

Functional residual capacity (FRC(RV+ERV): The amount of air remaining in the lung after a normal expiration.

The level of FRC is a respiratory “mid-position”, which is determined by the mechanical balance of the thorax-lung complex: the lungs tend to contract because of their elasticity, the thorax tends to dilate – the balance of the two forces determines FRC. Any increase of FRC (e.g. loss of elastic fibers) weakens the efficacy of respiration, since in the large volume the 21% oxygen of the normally inspired VT cannot increase alveolar oxygen tension sufficiently. Besides, due to enlargement of airways and alveoli only a smaller part of the gas is in direct contact with the alveolar wall (large functional dead-space).

Gas-dilution method, with He of poor diffusibility was used earlier to determine FRC: after a normal expiration the patient respired 10% He-containing air from a bell-spirometer until equilibration of He-concentrations in the bell and the expired air – the FRC was calculated from the dilution of He (according to the rule of Boyle-Mariotte). With this method the poorly ventilated closed compartments (e.g. bullas in emphysema) were not measured. Recently body-plethysmography is used to measure the total air content in the thorax at the end of a quiet expiration, including the air trapped beyond the obstruction – in obstructive diseases this method may give much larger FRC values than the dilution method. Instead of FRC, the value determined by body-plethysmography is better called intrathoracic gas volume (ITGV). At ITGV-measurement the airflow is closed by a bolt for 2-3 sec at the mouth-piece, whilst the patient performs rapid small respiratory movements. Upon closing the airflow, the pressures equilibrate in the airways and the pressure at the mouth is the same as in the alveoli. At inspiration the thorax distends, the pressure at the mouth (and the alveoli) decreases, while in the cabin the volume of the air decreases and its pressure increases. According to the rule of Boyle-Mariotte (= pressure multiplied by volume is standard for gases of the same amount and temperature) the ratio between the pressures at the mouth and in the cabin can be used to calculate the ITGV (calculation is done by the computer).

Normally FRC and ITGV are equal, in obstructive respiratory diseases ITGV > FRC.

A.2.1.1.2. DYNAMIC PARAMETERS:

The dynamic parameters depend on time. They are measured in the course of forced expiration or inspiration. Their pathological changes suggest presence of obstructive respiratory disorder.

Fig. A2.2.

Forced expiratory capacity (forced vitalcapacity FVC): The air amount that can be expired by force from maximal inspiratory position. Normally it is almost equal with VC, in airway obstruction it is much smaller than VC.

Forced expiratory volume (FEV1): The volume of expired air (starting from total capacity) within the first second of a forced expiration.

Tiffeneau index (FEV1/VC%): It shows whether or not decreases in VC and in FEV1 are proportional. It is useful in the differentiation of obstructive and restrictive disorders: a decrease of Tiffeneau index (<75%) suggests airway obstruction.

Forced inspiratory volume (FIV1): The volume that can be inspired by maximal force within a second, following a maximal expiration. In healthy persons and patients with intrathoracic obstruction it is greater than FEV1. During inspiration the brochi are pulled open by the negative intrathoracic pressure, but they are narrower during the positive intrathoracic pressure of expiration. If FIV1 decreases more than FEV1, it suggests narrowing of upper airways or weakness of respiratory muscles.

Maximal mean expiratory flow (MMEF): This is the ratio of the 25-75% of FVC volume and the time needed for this volume change. It is a more sensitive indicator of airway obstruction than FEV1, and suggests obstruction of small airways (<2 mm) with normal VC, FEV1 and Raw values.

A.2.1.1.3. FLOW-VOLUME CURVES:

Such curves may be analyzed under static and dynamic circumstances. The dynamic ones (flow-volume curves during forced expiration and inspiration) have diagnostic value.

Fig. A2.3.

During forced expiration the flow speed decreases both if retraction forces are weaker and if airway narrowing is present. The first part of forced expiration depends on the cooperation of the patient, because it depends on the force exerted by the patient. The lower parts depend on the airway resistance (does not depend on cooperation). In cooperating patients narrowing of the large airways is suggested by decreased PEF and MEF75, while smaller MEF25 and MEF50 indicate damage of small airways.

A.2.1.1.4. AIRWAY RESISTANCE (RAW):

This is a parameter of the mechanics of respiration. It shows the extent of the change in the alveolar pressure that is needed for a unit change of flow speed. It is necessary to measure flow speed and alveolar pressure changes. The patient breathes quietly in the cabin of the body-plethysmograph. At inspiration the low alveolar pressure allows airflow into the lung, the thoracic and lung volumes increase, compressing the air in the cabin and increasing its pressure. The opposite process happens at expiration. From changes in cabin pressure conclusions can be drawn on the alveolar pressure changes that induced the air flow.

The normal airway resistance is below 0.3 kPa/liter/sec.

Narrowing of the airways enhances, their dilatation decreases the resistance. The trachea has the largest diameter (cross-section of 2 cm2), the total cross-section of the airways quickly increases until the alveoli (60-100 m2). Therefore, even a moderate obstruction of the big airways causes a significant increase of resistance. A rise of Raw value represents the narrowing of mainly the large airways. In healthy persons the small airways account for only 10-20% of total airway resistance). The resistance may remain near-normal even if they are severely narrowed – therefore it will be important an early analysis of the parameters of small airway obstruction.

For checking the efficacy of medication the specific gauge (conduction capacity) of airways (sGaw) is commonly used, which is a reciprocal value of specific resistance (Raw x ITGV). For an increase in airway resistance, the respiratory mid-position is shifted up (ITGV rises) – at larger volume even the bronchial diameter increases slightly and the airway resistance decreases. After this, bronchodilators induce virtually no decrease in airway resistance. The explanation is that with decreasing Raw the compensatory rise in mid-position (TGV) also decreases. At this smaller volume the bronchi become narrower again and the resistance increases. The drugs may cause only minimal decrease of resistance, but TGV decreases and the complaints of the patient improve. This emphasizes the importance of sGaw at tests of drugs.

A.2.1.1.5. COMPLIANCE

This parameter of respiratory mechanics shows the amount of air flowing into (or out of) the lung upon unit change in transpulmonary pressure (difference of pressure at the mouth and in the pleural cavity).

Static compliance is more informative, since under dynamic conditions the resistance derived from flow and friction influence the results of lung compliance. In tests of static compliance, the respiratory movements have to be interrupted several times during inspiration and expiration, and compliance is measured during the arrests. Changes of the intrapleural pressure are identical with pressure change sin the esophagus, which in turn, van be measures by a cannula introduced into the esophagus.

Normally the compliance is 2 liters/kPa, i.e. 1 kPa pressure change results in inflow (or outflow) of 2 liters of air into (or out of) the lung. Lung compliance markedly decreases in pulmonary fibroses: due to rigidity of the lung similar pressure changes can induce much smaller air flow. Thus, the compliance value may decrease to 0.2 liters/kPa. Cardiac congestion also decreases lung distensibility. In contrast, in emphysema the distensibility is high, compliance may be 4-5-times higher than normally, since the retraction force diminishes.

A.2.1.2. EVALUATION OF VENTILATION PARAMETERS

At evaluation it can be decided whether the respiratory parameters of the patient are normal or pathological. In pathological cases it has to be determined whether the disorders are of restrictive, obstructive, or mixed (restrictive + obstructive) nature, and the severity of the situation can also be diagnosed. With repeated tests the changes of functional parameters can be evaluated, as well as the efficacy of the applied therapy.

A.2.1.2.1. RESTRICTIVE RESPIRATORY DISORDERS

Restrictive respiratory disorder is caused by any state with decreased respiratory surface, e.g. inflammation, pneumonia, cardiac failure, tumor, compression of the lung by pleural fluid, atelectasis due to airway obstruction, occlusion of the alveoli (pulmonary edema). Restrictive disorders develop also pulmonary fibroses that induce rigidity of the lung, or diseases inhibiting thoracic movements (muscle abnormalities, kyphoscoliosis).

Ventilation disorder of restrictive nature is characterized by decrease of static volumes of the lung. VC, TLC, RV and FRC decrease by about the same degree. Changes of static values are followed by proportional changes of dynamic parameters (FVC, FEV1), therefore the Tiffeneau index remains normal.

A.2.1.2.2. OBSTRUCTIVE RESPIRATORY DISORDERS

Narrowing of the bronchial system may develop due to endobronchial or exobronchial mechanisms.

The endobronchial obstruction in the background of bronchial asthma or chronic bronchitis is caused by swelling (edema, inflammatory infiltration) of the bronchial mucosa, by spasm of bronchial smooth muscles and by enhanced secretion of mucus. Among the functional findings typical is the decrease in FEV1 and Tiffeneau index as well as the rise in airway resistance.

Exobronchial obstruction is characteristic for patients with emphysema. Here obstruction is caused by collapse of bronchi in the course of forced expiration. This may be explained by loss of elastic fibers. The retraction force of these fibers should pull open the bronchioles during expiration, counteracting the high intrathoracic pressure (which would tend to compress the bronchioles). It follows that a marked decrease of dynamic parameters (FEV1, Tiffeneau-index, FVC) can be observed in patients with emphysema, but during quiet respiration the airway resistance is normal (without elevation of the intrathoracic pressure there is hardly any airway obstruction). The significant rise of FRC is caused by air trapping because of bronchial collapse.

A.2.1.2.3. MIXED (RESTRICTIVE AND OBSTRUCTIVE) DISORDERS

The two types of disorders can be seen together. It occurs in cases of combination of the two types of disease, e.g. pulmonary fibrosis in patients with bronchitis, or pulmonary resection in patients with bronchitis.

A.2.1.2.4. EXTRA- AND INTRA-THORACIC AIRWAY OBSTRUCTION

I. Extrathoracic airway obstruction

Extrathoracic airway obstruction may be caused by laryngeal tumor, Reinke-edema, large goiter, and state after tracheostomy. Respiratory functional signs suggesting extrathoracal obstruction: the inspiratory part of the flow-volume curve exhibits a plateau-like breakage, or if FEV1> FIV1.

A.2.1.2.5. ANALYSIS OF DRUG-EFFECTS

According to their response to short-action (2-agonists, airway obstructions may be divided into reversible, and irreversible groups (bronchial asthma is reversible, while chronic bronchitis is rather irreversible). At the first respiratory test the presence of airway obstruction should be verified, then the patient is given a short-action inhalatory  (2 mimetic. 15-30 min later the test is repeated. If the FEV1 value improves (at least by 12%, or minimum 200 ml), the airway obstruction is reversible. This helps in the differential diagnosis and in establishing a therapy.

A.2.2. ANALYSIS OF DIFFUSION

The diffusion analysis provides information about the diffusion of oxygen from the alveolar gas to red blood cells and the diffusion of CO2 in opposite direction.

The most common method is the single-breath CO inhalation test: after maximal expiration the patient inhales a mixture containing 0.3% CO and 10% He until TLC level, keeps the mixture in the lung for 10 s, then slow expiration follows. Diffusion capacity can be calculated from the exhaled CO and He concentrations. (CO has a similar diffusibility as O2, but its affinity to Hb is 210-times greater. Besides, normally there is no CO in the venous blood).  The fall of CO-concentration in the exhaled air is partly due to diffusion through the alveolo-capillary membrane, partly to dilution in RV. The RV can be calculated from He dilution, since He cannot diffuse through the membrane.

Diffusion capacity (transfer factor) is the gas volume which, by the alveolo-capillary pressure gradient, can get to the blood of pulmonary capillaries within a minute. It is proportional with the alveolo-capillary pressure gradient, the size of the diffusion surface and it is inversely related to the length of diffusion route.

Fig. A2.8.

The diffusion capacity decreases e.g. in emphysema due to destroyed alveolo-capillary membranes, or in pulmonary fibroses due to thickening of the membrane.

Diffusion (transfer) coefficient: this informs about the diffusion per 1 liter of pulmonary volume.

In patients with emphysema (membrane damage) the diffusion coefficient is low, while in those patients with decreased diffusion capacity due to removal a part of the lung (and decrease of diffusion surface) the diffusion coefficient remains normal in the remaining intact lung.

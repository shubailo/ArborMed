Energy sources (in a man of 75 kg)

a)	- ATP: 1.2 kcal (sufficient for the first 2-3 seconds of work)

- Creatine-phosphate: 3.6 kcal (enough for first 5-6 seconds of work)

– anaerobic glucose utilization (glucose – pyruvate – lactic acid; 2ATP): This early anaerobic breakdown of glucose is half as quick as the breakdown of macroergic phosphates, but faster than the oxidative phosphorylation (NB: only carbohydrates can be burnt anaerobically). This helps the muscles through the initial O2 deficient state. O2-debt accumulates in this period. In a 100-m, 10 sec short-distance running 85% of the necessary energy is derived from anaerobic metabolism. In a running of 2-miles (or 10-min) this is only 20% and in a 60-min long-distance running only 5%.

c)	- aerobic metabolic system: This is slow, but when it starts operating, enables steady exercise. It is not known, what switches on the aerobic system.

Glucose-breakdown: C6H12O6 + 6O2 = 6CO2 + 12H2O + 36ATP, i.e. the RQ (CO2/O2) = 1.0. A 75 kg man has about 20 kg muscle. This amount of muscle contains glycogen corresponding to about 1200 kcal, i.e. ca. 1200 kcal carbohydrate can be used only locally (muscle glycogen cannot be converted to blood glucose due to lack of glucose-6-phosphatase enzyme in the muscle). The utilized blood glucose can be replaced by breakdown of the 70-90 g hepatic glycogen content.

Palmitic acid breakdown: C16H32O2 + 23O2 = 16CO2 + 16H2O + 130ATP, RQ (CO2/O2) = 0.7. The fat content of the skeletal muscle corresponds to ca. 20 g triglyceride/kg wet muscle, i.e. ca. 9-times higher energy density and energy content as compared with carbohydrates (its value in a 75 kg person is ca. 5.000 kcal). In addition, ca. 15 kg extramuscular fat stores ca. 135 000 kcal energy. Burning fat produces relatively more ATP and CO2, and uses relatively more oxygen (RQ is low) than the carbohydrate utilization.

d)     - beyond certain intensity or duration of work, a repeated anaerobic phase follows. In these cases, the anaerobic processes continuously go on, parallel with the aerobic ones (see anaerobic threshold). Utilization of carbohydrates speeds up, and the reserves are quickly exhausted – this contributes to the feeling of tiredness.

A3. 1.1. ANAEROBIC THRESHOLD

In the early phase of activity and later again, beyond a certain work load, the working muscle makes use of anaerobic energy-producing processes. In the course of this, lactate is produced. By buffering lactate, CO2 is released from bicarbonate and this CO2 is added to that produced in the metabolic processes. Accordingly, the ratio of CO2/O2 (RQ) may be greater than 1.0, but this is not a real RQ – a part of CO2 originates from buffering. (The CO2/O2 ratio measured from the exhaled air during exercise is named respiratory exchange rate /RER/). The possible extent of anaerobic energy production is limited (30 kcal). Therefore, the „anaerobic threshold” or „lactate acidosis threshold” (LAT) foretells the exhaustion of muscles, and it informs about the possible limit the patient can bear. The greater the work load (oxygen consumption) where the anaerobic threshold is reached, the more trained and healthy is the patient. [NB: in fact, the anaerobic threshold is not a point, rather a gradual transition. With increasing work load, the anaerobic metabolism takes an increasing share of total energy production. At the beginnings the increasing lactate production can easily be compensated. The first noticeable sign of reaching the anaerobic threshold is the rise in plasma lactate level and the consequent CO2 expiration due to buffering.  The pH starts decreasing only when the lactate production exceeds the buffer capacity. Finally, the respiratory compensation of metabolic acidosis leads to enhanced ventilation, hypocapnia develops and increases – the earlier standard – total ventilation (VE)/CO2 ratio.) Reaching the anaerobic threshold does not mean discontinuation of the aerobic metabolism. Still, above the anaerobic threshold sustained physical work is not possible (see LAT in Fig. A3.2.)].

Accordingly, the level of work load where anaerobic metabolism is „switched on” may be characterized with the corresponding O2-consumption. The working O2-consumption, in turn, can be expressed as multiplicative factor of resting metabolism. Its measure is MET = 3.5-3.6 ml/kg/min. In trained men anaerobic threshold is reached at a load of 10-MET, in sedentary people at 4-MET, in heart diseases only 2-MET.

Lactate is not toxic, although it causes acidosis. In sportsmen lactate acidosis may reach pH 7.0, with lactate concentration of 10 mmol/liter. Physiological effects of lactate: 1) it causes vasodilation, 2) by Bohr-effect it shifts the Hb oxygen-saturation curve to the right and increases oxygen extraction in tissues, 3) it stimulates respiration, 4) it enhances oxygen need (for regeneration of lactate in gluconeogenesis, oxygen is needed), 5) it decreases the efficacy of muscle activity.

*MET = metabolic unit. At rest the oxygen consumption of healthy adults is ca. 250 ml/min. This value is similar in sportsmen and in sedentary people. This value related to body weight (250/70) gives the value of MET: about 3.6 ml/kg/min. The intensity of an exercise can be characterized by the oxygen consumption needed to perform it. A work intensity of 2-MET elevates oxygen consumption twice above the resting level, a work load of 10-MET elevates it 10-fold. (ch. A8.).

A3. 1.2. MAXIMAL OXYGEN CONSUMPTION (VO2 MAX)

During physical exercise the O2 need of muscles increases immediately, while the circulation and O2 supply can increase only gradually. This results in O2-debt (Fig. A3.3.).

Fig. A3.3.

With increasing loads (until certain limits), the shape of the curve remains standard. At further loads (normally from 200 watts), steady state cannot develop (Fig. A3.4.), the O2-need and with it the cardiac output continuously increases until the maximal capacity. Having reached the VO2max, from 250 watts the VO2 does not increase. This is where the anaerobic threshold can be demonstrated, the lactate levels quickly rise, and the possible duration of further exercise decreases.

The following factors contribute to the development of O2-debt:

- O2 is needed for replacement of the macroergic phosphates that were exhausted at the beginning of exercise.

- O2 is needed to re-transform lactate to glucose (gluconeogenesis)

- the rising core temperature (even 3(C) induces higher metabolism

- 10% of oxygen is needed to cover mechanisms of the high cardiac output (e.g. 			vasoconstriction at certain regions)

- 2-5 % of this O2 serves to fill up O2 stores (e.g. myoglobin)

- respiratory muscles have a higher O2 need

- energy is needed to replace lost K, Na, Ca

- O2 is needed to cover the myocardial O2 need enhanced by the circulating 			catecholamines (high frequency and enhanced contractility)

Fig. A3.4.

The largest oxygen consumption that can be reached with exercise is the maximal oxygen consumption, VO2 max. VO2 max is a reliable parameter of a person’s physical capabilities.

Maximal O2-consumption is determined by:

- hereditary factors

- sex (it is higher in men)

- age (maximum is reached at age 20, then a 2%/year decrease follows)

- training state: the average resting O2-consumption is 250 ml/min, VO2 max is 3600 ml/min (sedentary lifestyle), it may even 5.100 ml/min (in marathon runners), but 3-wks bed-rest may decrease it by 20-25%, short-term training may increase it by 20%.

The anaerobic threshold is usually at about 50-60% of VO2 max. This may also change with training level, e.g. in ski-racers it may be at 80%.

A3.1.3. RESPIRATORY ADAPTATION AND ITS DISORDERS IN EXERCISE

Upon the cortical irradiation (increased sympathetic tone preceding exercise and during it), and through the effect of neural afferents from muscles and joints, the ventilation increases. With deep breaths the dead-space ventilation decreases (from a resting value of ca. 30% even to 10%). Pulmonary blood flow improves, the V/Q ratio approaches 1, the diffusion capacity becomes better. Despite enhanced O2-consumption and CO2-production the blood gas tensions remain normal (Fig. A3.5., continuous line).

Example (Fig.A3.5.):

During an exercise with 2 liters/min O2 consumption (VO2) a healthy person (shown by continuous line in the Fig.) remains below the anaerobic threshold. Thus, RQ 0.85 is maintained (in upper right quadrant it crosses the 0.85 axis, the CO2 tension and CO2 sensitivity are normal (in upper left quadrant the 40 mmHg axis is reached), and the functional dead-space is low (the 0.1 axis is crossed in lower left quadrant). Accordingly, the necessary ventilation (VE) is 40 liter/min.

With similar O2 need of 2 liters/min an obstructive pulmonary patient (shown by interrupted line) is already above the anaerobic threshold (RQ = 1 in upper right axis), the CO2 sensitivity is high, on left side the 30 mmHg CO2 axis is crossed, the functional dead-space is large (in lower left quadrant VD/VT = 0,5). Accordingly, the necessary ventilation is 100-120 liter/min to reach the same 2 liters/min VO2. The dotted line running nearest crossing of the axes shows the characteristics of a trained athlete.

The maximal ventilation upon exercise (VE max) depends on:

- VCO2 (CO2 production by aerobic and anaerobic ways)

- CO2 tension of arterial blood

- ratio of dead-space (VD) and tidal volume (VT) (normally 20-30%)

VEmax usually cannot reach the maximal voluntary ventilation (MVV), since the physical performance (thus also the VEmax) is generally limited by the cardiovascular system. (As an exception, in leading sportsmen the VEmax may reach or exceed MVV.) Breathing reserve (BR) = (difference of MVV and VEmax) = 150-200 liters/min – 100 liters/min. In patients with obstructive and restrictive pulmonary diseases the breathing reserve becomes smaller or almost zero (ventilation can hardly be enhanced by either exercise or voluntarily). Decreased breathing reserve indicates that these patients must stop exercise due to very soon appearing dyspnea (while in healthy persons the exercise limit is determined by the circulatory system).

OBSTRUCTIVE RESPIRATORY DISORDER (COPD) (see Fig. A3.6.)

- Main features of COPD (ch. 3.1.2.2.) are obstruction of small airways, enhanced airway resistance and consequently difficult and prolonged expiration. The patients tend to take deep and rare breaths, because this means smaller effort: with higher respiratory rate the air turbulence would further increase the already high airway resistance. The total work of respiration is increased (in emphysema, besides obstruction, the expiratory work grows also because of loss of elastic fibers), and these induce the feeling of dyspnea. Since during an increased ventilation need the patient cannot quickly exhale the large amount of inhaled air, increasing amounts of air remain in the lungs and dynamic hyperinflation develops. It is difficult to further increase the size of the bloated thorax (the length of the respiratory fibers is not optimal), therefore the patient feels extreme dyspnea, exhaustion, and the exercise must be finished.

- VO2 max (and the work-capacity) is low.

- MVV is markedly decreased.

- The breathing reserve (difference of MVV and VEmax) is strongly reduced or totally absent. The importance of this is that, while in healthy persons the circulatory system limits exercise capacity, in these patients, exercise is interrupted rather soon, because of dyspnea.

- The anaerobic threshold of the patients is low, because they are untrained, and the dyspnea makes them avoid any physical activity. If work is still done, the anaerobic threshold may not be reached, since unbearable dyspnea occurs earlier.

Fig. A3.6.

- Due to ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) mismatching (shunting, ch. 3.2.1.) the ventilation is ineffective, similar O2 need can be realized by much increased total ventilation. Thus, ventilation is high already at rest, to keep CO2 level normal (there is tendency for hypoxia and hypercapnia at rest).

- Respiratory rate (due to difficult and prolonged expiration) characteristically remains below 50/min.

- The VT/IRV ratio (tidal volume/inspiratory reserve) is normal, about 80%.

- Well-planned, gradual training program may improve the anaerobic threshold even in such patients: enhancement of CO2 production starts at greater work loads thus, the patients start becoming dyspneic only at higher loads and they may be able to perform their everyday physical activities (climbing steps, walking, housework, etc.) more easily.

RESTRICTIVE DISORDER

- The lung is small and rigid (TLC is small, compliance is low, the elastic resistance is very high), superficial, frequent breaths characterize these patients. VT increase is moderate during exercise. Respiratory rates quickly rise, even easily above 50/min. Due to superficial nature of ventilation, the ratio of dead-space ventilation (VD/VT) is very high.

- VO2 max (and working capacity) is low.

- MVV is significantly reduced.

- The breathing reserve (difference of MVV and VEmax) is greatly reduced or totally absent, similarly as in COPD.

- The anaerobic threshold of the patients (as in COPD) is low, due to the untrained state. During eventual work-loads the patients may not reach the anaerobic threshold: they feel dyspnea sooner.

- V/Q mismatching is also present (fibrosis destroys capillaries), and the functional dead-space is large.

- Since there is fibrosis around the capillaries, the diffusion capacity is small, and the arterial O2 tension is low. At the beginning hypoxia develops only due to exercise-induced shortening of the transit time, later hypoxia (latent diffusion disorder) is present at rest, too. The hypoxia becomes severe much earlier than in COPD patients.

- Respiratory rate may exceed 50/min.

- The VT/IRV ratio is very high.

- Well-planned, gradual training program may improve the anaerobic threshold even in such patients: enhancement of CO2 production starts at greater work-loads thus, the patients start becoming dyspneic only at high loads and they may be able to perform the everyday physical activities (climbing steps, housework, etc.) more easily.

A common feature of the two abnormalities is that at maximal exercise, due to the massive increase of respiratory work, the oxygen need of the respiratory muscles is very high. While in exercising healthy persons the maximal oxygen need of the respiratory muscles is 9 ml/100g/min, this value may reach 15 ml/100g/min in both abnormalities.

In exercise, the resting cardiac output of 5 liters/min may increase 4-5-fold, in sportsmen, it may even reach 40 liters/min. (Cardiac output starts increasing already before the onset of activity: probably psychological factors and the early increase in sympathetic tone may explain this observation). Cardiac output is defined as a product of heart rate multiplication by stroke volume.

Heart rate – upon the increase in sympathetic tone and circulating catecholamines – may increase from the resting 60-80/min to the maximal 220-age (or 210-0.662xage; i.e. at the age of 65 to about 160 beats/min), this possibly results in nearly 3-fold increase in cardiac output. However, the rising frequency has some negative effects as well: the diastolic time is shortened, thus, the coronary blood flow decreases and the myocardium may become hypoxic, despite an enhanced oxygen need. In trained young men, the rise in heart rate at the start of exercise is indeed moderate, and in their adaptation, the stroke volume has a relatively greater role. However, during intensive work, the heart rate may reach maximal levels: since their frequency reserve is high, it may increase from 50/min to 200/min (4-times higher cardiac output).

Stroke volume (SV) can increase from the resting value of 70-80 ml to or above a maximum of 110-140 ml. It is determined by the preload, contractility and afterload.

Preload depends mainly in venous return and filling state of blood reservoires (e.g. splanchnic vessels, spleen). The enhanced sympathetic activity during physical work and the pump-activity of muscles enhance venous return and emptying of the reservoires. (Higher venous tone is brought about by sympathetic activity, and muscle pump helps venous return from splanchnic regions, skin and kidneys, while the inactive muscles receive less blood due to redistribution of circulation.) Consequently, both EDV and EDp increase, the wall tension is higher, and the Frank-Starling mechanism allows an increase in stroke volume even without changing contractility (however, the coronary blood flow worsens, because of the high EDp). The preload is also influenced, among others, by the amount of circulating blood, the intrathoracic pressure (this may be decreased by respiratory excursions of the thorax /negative intrathoracic pressure during inspiration/, and increased by fluid accumulation or PTX), and the distensibility of the ventricular wall.

The contractility of the myocardium is increased by the sympathetic tone (through (1-receptors of the myocardium) and by the circulating catecholamines (positive inotropic effect). Accordingly, the ejection fraction increases (EF = ratio of SV and EDV).

The afterload is determined mainly by the total peripheral resistance (TPR). In exercise, the TPR decreases: although the high sympathetic tone, per se, would cause vasoconstriction and high TPR, the massive vasodilation in the working muscles lead to a net fall in TPR. Vasodilation develops only in loco, in the working muscles, due to locally accumulating metabolic products (CO2, AMP, acidosis, K, heat, etc.).

Taken together, during exercise the heart rate increases (the more trained the person, the more moderate the increase), SV increases and systolic pressure rises – diastolic pressure decreases at the beginning, later mild elevation is possible (TPR fall). Quickly rising diastolic pressure (or disproportionally high systolic pressure upon moderate exercise) suggests presence of hypertension disease. Otherwise in young trained men at maximal exercise a systolic pressure of ≥200 mmHg is not a rarity.

Although it is not directly a change of circulation, it is worth mentioning here that the ΔAVO2 may increase about 3-fold in exercise. The tissue oxygen utilization is not the usual 5% (out of 20% arterial oxygen content), but it may be 3-times higher – this may partly be explained by redistribution of blood flow.

In patients of compromised heart function, the rise of cardiac output lags behind that of the healthy persons. In extreme cases no rise is possible above the resting level.

Stoke volume can increase only a little, if at all. In various disorders either the preload or the contractility is defective, or the afterload may increase. The sympathetic activity is high already at rest, and this tends to elevate heart rate and TPR along with abnormal distribution of circulation (in the kidney and splanchnic region there is really vasoconstriction). Vasodilation develops only in working muscles (and skin, to lose heat), as a consequence of locally accumulating metabolites (CO2, AMP, adenosine, acidosis, K+, NO, heat, etc.). In healthy persons these processes would lead to decrease of diastolic pressure during exercise, but not in the case of failing heart.

Characteristically, heart rate quickly increases even upon moderate exercise (the more trained the person is, the slower the rise of heart rate), as if compensating the lack of SV-rise. In order to produce some elevation of stroke volume the Frank-Starling mechanism is of utmost importance, therefore the increase of EDV and EDp can soon occur. While in healthy people and in athletes with increasing exercise the stroke volume first grows linearly then it stagnates, in cardiac patients an early mild rise may be followed even by a fall in stroke volume.

The total peripheral resistance is continuously higher than in healthy people, and it does not decrease during exercise, therefore both systolic and diastolic pressures quickly rise (exception is mitral stenosis, with hypotension upon work).

The ΔAVO2 is higher already at rest, because of the permanently slow perfusion and tissue acidosis. The reserve capacity, which might be used in work, is small.

During exercise of cardiac patients:

- tachycardia develops soon – myocardial hypoxia – angina pectoris

- contractility of the heart cannot be increased sufficiently – due to high preload the ventricle dilates and with time this suppresses contractility

- local metabolites accumulate during work – the local vasodilation is maintained, but it   is more moderate in the elderly and heart failure patients, with more frequent atherosclerosis and high sympathetic tone

- due to poor O2-supply, the ratio of anaerobic work quickly increases – lactate accumulates and glycogen stores become exhausted – soon tiredness/exhaustion occurs (the maximal O2-consumption is low)

- due to rising EDV and EDp, pulmonary congestion develops – dyspnea: the compliance of damaged, fibrotic myocardium decreases, therefore the EDV rise needs disproportionately high EDp rise. As a result, quickly, severe dyspnea develops.

A3.3. ANALYSIS OF THE FUNCTIONAL CAPACITY OF THE CARDIORESPIRATORY SYSTEM (SPIROERGOMETRY) AND ITS IMPORTANCE

By the help of treadmill or bicycle ergometer, spiroergometry analyses the effects of precisely defined intensity work-loads on the cardiovascular and respiratory systems in healthy and sick persons. Measurements are performed in sitting and standing positions, and then the loads are gradually increased in small steps, until some indication occurs for interruption or a given time elapses (e.g. 20 min at demonstration).

GENERAL FEATURES OF SPIROERGOMETRIC EXAMINATIONS:

- The intention is to activate big groups of muscle – there is no need of special faculties of movement

- Gradually increasing load is applied, starting from a minimal level – it can be adapted to individual capabilities

- Safe and not inconvenient/overburdening for the patient – repeated examinations are possible

- Relatively short time is enough to perform it

- The results of measurements can be well-standardized. Normal values for various age-groups and populations are available

- During rest and exercise the cardiac and respiratory functions are examined parallel

Indications of spiroergometric examination:

- Respiratory indications:	- COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases, e.g. 	chronic bronchitis, emphysema)

- restrictive pulmonary diseases

- primary and secondary pulmonary hypertension

- right-to-left shunt

- congenital or acquired myopathies

- Cardiovascular indications: - coronary diseases (diagnosis)

- assessing possible work-load of known cardiac patients (coronary disease, heart malformations, cardiomyopathies)

- assessment of course of AMI or state of coronary revascularization

- clarification of angina pectoris or similar complaints

- Other indications: 	- evaluation of training programs in sportsmen

- also applied for research purposes

The risk of a spiroergometric examination is relatively small, but lethal complication is 1/10.000 examination, and rhythm abnormalities, AMI may occur. Therefore, it is preceded by a physical examination and a 12-channel ECG. The examiner team must be prepared for reanimation.

The measured parameters during spiroergometric examination:

- blood pressure in every minute

- continuous monitoring of ECG (preferably 12-channels) and heart rate

- total ventilation

- oxygen consumption and CO2 production

- intensity and time of work-load

- blood lactate and pH parameters measured occasionally

The information possibly gained from the examination:

1. Work-capacity – fitness:

- anaerobic threshold

- VO2 max

2. Cardiovascular information:

- work-load limit for appearance of angina or dyspnea

- extent of rise in heart rate (too high or insufficient), development of ST depression

- changes in blood pressure

- it is characteristic if VO2 does not increase, due to low anaerobic threshold

3. Information on respiratory system:

- high ventilation need for unit VO2 (high ventilation equivalent), suggests a V/Q mismatching with increased functional dead-space

- great difference between the alveolar (end-tidal) and arterial CO2 tensions suggests disorder of alveolo-capillary diffusion

Indications of interruption of the examination:

- angina, or >2-3 mm (particularly horizontal or descending) ST depression/elevation

- severe arrhythmia, multiple VES, conduction disorders (e.g. functional right bundle branch block)

- reaching maximal heart rate for age

- severe dyspnea

- exhaustion, severe tiredness

- very high (>260 mmHg systolic, >130 mmHg diastolic) blood pressure, or development of very low blood pressure

Absolute contraindications of the examination:

- acute febrile illness

- ≤5-day-old AMI

- acute left ventricular failure

- complex arrhythmia

- pulmonary edema

- very high blood pressure in the preceding days (ca. 200 mmHg)

- bronchial asthma attack in preceding days, or exercise-induced bronchial spasm

- myocardial inflammations

- instable angina in the preceding 3 days

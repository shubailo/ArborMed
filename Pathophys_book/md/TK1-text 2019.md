INTRODUCTION TO THE BASIC CONCEPTS OF PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

1.1. PATHOPHYSIOLOGY: DEFINITIONS

The science of physiology imparts a sense of normalcy among the various functions of the living organism, or some organs and organ systems. However, these functions are consistently manifested in forms influenced by external and internal factors, therefore, they are not strictly standardized. The alteration of functions, the nature or extent of which are pathological, are defined as dysfunctions; among the most severe forms, specifically, once a function cannot fulfill its biological role, they are referred to as functional insufficiencies or failures. Pathophysiology embodies the various pathologically altered functions. The philosophers question regarding physiology is poised in the following manner; ”How does the body function?” Contrast with the supportive side, regarding pathophysiology; ”How does the body malfunction?”

Specifically, regarding certain functional changes, the body attempts to adapt through methods evolving from phylogeny, which subsequently, seems ”appropriate”. The reliance upon other physiological mechanisms, implies that the original change may be compensated and the final biological ”aim” of the function, thus secured. In cases of prolonged influence upon physiological functions, the operational mode of a number of interacting systems may be modified, establishing a new balance between the original functions and these mechanisms, allowing a complex adaptation to the initial influences. Without compensation and shorter or longer periods of adaptation, in consideration of the individual systems, severe dysfunctions will likely develop even upon minor influencing effects. The occurrence of hypoxia in the highest elevations throughout the world, for example, can be quickly compensated to a certain extent by enhanced respiration and circulation, however, in sustained adaptations the burden for circulation lessens, since the red blood cell formation also increases, which, in turn, necessitates normal renal and endocrine function, etc., and, obviously, without these, the state of hypoxia will be extremely severe.

The balanced operation of physiological functions implies an assumption towards some form of regulation/counter-regulation. Apart from the central elements of each regulation, this necessitates afferent information (e.g. thermoreceptors) from the regulated system (e.g. body temperature), and also requires adequate efferent mechanisms (e.g. mechanisms of heat production and heat loss). Since all the functioning elements of the regulation are under constant influence from a combination of external factors and internal factors from various organ systems, the (negative) feedback signals are in perpetual change, the regulated component is not strictly standardized, yet shows smaller or greater variations or imbalances adjacent to a certain value (e.g. physiological changes of blood glucose or body temperature near a certain virtual ”set-point”). Other regulatory methods are also known in physiology and pathophysiology e.g. feed-forward circles, such as seen in blood coagulation, in inflammatory processes, and/or in the hyperfiltration-induced glomerular damage of the kidneys (e.g in diabetic nephropathy), etc.

Due to disorders of the incoming information or its processing, the regulated value may differ from the normal value, consequently centrally regulated and coordinated changes may be seen in the given function (e.g. centrally regulated elevation of body temperature, as in the case of a fever), without compensatory mechanisms. In other cases, the body attempts to compensate the factor by placing a load upon the organism, however, the burden is so heavy that even maximal compensation cannot re-establish the normal functions and the given function passively changes (e.g. passive elevation of body temperature upon extreme heat load results in hyperthermia).

It must be emphasized that the operations among individual homeostatic systems are interrelated, they interact. The primary load to any one of these systems or its functional change in compensation or adaptation unavoidably and inexplicably, affects the function of a variety of other systems (e.g. interactions between thermoregulation, circulation and salt/water balance with endocrine background), undoubtedly suggesting, all systems should be investigated regarding their vast, complex interactions.

The objective of pathophysiology is to confront the dysfunctions or complete functional insufficiencies of organs and organ systems, and to examine the corresponding steps of compensation and the realm of adaptation. Furthermore, pathophysiology investigates various pathological changes amongst those distinct interactions between the organ systems which affect the entire body, and, simultaneously, showing or proposing possible (theoretical) means of prevention and therapy. The persistent questions, ”why” and ”how” beg a logical and relevant explanation.

To fulfill this task, pathophysiology relies upon the various interconnected aspects of pathological changes throughout organs and organ systems, diseases and subsequently, their prescribed treatments:

1) Etiology of a disease, its evoking factors (e.g. exogenous/endogenous triggering factors which lead to a possible injury of pancreatic β-cells)

2) Steps of pathogenesis (e.g. pancreatic changes upon the effect of these etiological factors)

3) Pathomechanism of the given disease (e.g. changes in the body and, consequently the injury of β-cells, and the biochemical process of diabetes mellitus)

4) Clinical manifestations of the disease, its course and consequences (e.g. the most important fundamental clinical symptoms and consequences of diabetes)

5) Interactions with other organs and organ systems, regarding their compensatory and adaptive changes, and, inevitably, the fierce competition among other homeostatic systems (e.g. diabetic ketoacidosis and its respiratory compensation)

6) Principles of prevention, slowing down the progression, effectively administering therapy, and ultimately the manifestation of a full recovery.

Disease is a dysfunction of some organ and/or organ system, of which the stage of dysfunction is currently in progress (and not a finished/stable pathological condition, such as, an amputation). It may appear in an acute form (which can be either cured, becomes increasingly chronic, and/or becomes fatal), or it may appear in a less quick subacute form versus an immediate form of prolonged chronic dysfunction. Constitutional and genetic traits of the patient, combined with the biological age, sex, acquired faculties and the environmental factors may all prove influential regarding the various characteristics of pathogenesis, pathomechanism, dynamics and naturally, the course of the disease.

Accordingly, the authors objective in support of this text, relies primarily upon the thorough knowledge of physiology and biochemistry, intertwined with fields of a host of other subjects such as anatomy and immunology, which, ideally, represents the acquired knowledge among preclinical medical students. In the implementation of these and the previous aspects, regarding its approach, this textbook highlights specific diseases, yet not purely from their clinical aspects, since its task is to shed light upon the functional background of the changes observed in the process of disease, rather than conveying information painstakingly organized with regards on the definite clinical and/or therapeutic data of any specific disease. Pathophysiology is involved in a multiplicity of problems, of which a single textbook proves insufficient when considering the vast spectrum of beneficial topics.

As clearly illustrated in the textbook’s title, the authors, somewhat subjectively, determined to provide a concise description of those basic pathophysiological concepts which may prove relevant and critically important in the general practice. A number of special pathological processes (glaucoma, epilepsy, presbyacusis, rheumatoid arthritis, impotence, sarcoidosis, porphyria, leukemia, etc.) characteristically all possess a pathophysiological background and, assuredly, we know something about the pathomechanism in all cases, however, these should be analyzed by the corresponding clinical disciplines and cannot be regarded as a basic pathophysiological problem. Similarly, the pathophysiology of the nervous or immune systems, or the onco-pathophysiology obviously exceed the present strategic plan. Nonetheless, a basic knowledge of pathophysiology may still prove immensely beneficial towards comprehending pathological phenomena throughout special diseases.

At the end of each chapter, a brief, concise review is offered regarding the given functional changes associated with the aging process. The usefulness of this emphasizes how the labyrinth of connections and interactions of the various functions can be demonstrated, of which, the gerontological changes can also be regarded as integrative pathophysiological changes. Additionally, in consideration of the 21st century, the status of health care among the aging population now has an immense role, as it is necessary to know increasingly far more in reference to the functional and pathophysiological characteristics among seniors, particularly those patients of an advanced age.

A7. THE METHODOLOGY OF ENERGY BALANCE

Fundamentals of the regulation of energy balance are discussed in Chapter 8. The following discourse includes a host of relevant methodology regarding the investigative characteristics of energy uptake, metabolism and balance.

The overall energy balance involves the intricate balance between the metabolic rate (MR and heat production) and heat loss, towards effectively maintain thermal stability. From another perspective, the balance of MR and food intake serves as a means in the governing of body weight regulation. Accordingly, the methods used in the analysis of energy balance encompass techniques in the investigations of the regulation of the metabolic rate, including the thermoregulation and the regulation of food intake.

A7.1. EXAMINATION OF METABOLIC RATE

METHODS OF ACUTE MEASUREMENTS OF THE ACTUAL METABOLIC RATE

Direct calorimetry:

Ultimately, all metabolic processes end with the production of calories of heat. If a subject (more often an experimental specimen) is placed into a double-walled, metabolic chamber, which is isolated from the outside, the heat, representing MR, shed from the specimen serves in the increase in the water’s temperature, which is perfused between the two walls. Provided that the chamber temperature is standard, it is possible to draw conclusion regarding the heat production, within a specific realm of time, i.e., for MR, from the rise of temperature of the water. The method is undoubtedly cumbersome and it is particularly difficult to apply in the case of human subjects.

Indirect calorimetry:

Calculations are based upon the gas exchange of the subject. If the calorie equivalent of the oxygen (= how many calories are produced by the utilization of 1 liter O2) is known, then, from the O2-consumption, the amount of produced calories may thus be calculated. However, depending on the nutrient actually burned, the calorie equivalent of O2 differs, as seen in the case of carbohydrates, in which it is 5-5.1 kcal/L, for fats it is 4.5-4.7 kcal/L, and betwixt these values, as in the case of mixed substrate combustion.

In reference to the actually combusted nutrients, a conclusion can be drawn regarding the respiratory quotient (RQ). RQ can be calculated from the produced CO2 and the simultaneously consumed O2: its value is 1.0 if carbohydrates are combusted, yet only 0.7 for fats and 0.85 for mixed nutrients. Accordingly, from RQ we can conclude the calorie equivalent of O2, which can be converted to calories (since the O2-consumption has already been measured in support of the calculation of RQ).

Measurements of O2-conssumption and CO2-production provide sufficient information for indirect determination of MR. In referencing such measurements, the experimental specimens are placed into a closed metabolic chamber of standard air flow (open circuit system). The chamber is kept at standard room temperature. In consideration of the air-flow rate and the difference of O2- and CO2-contents of the air entering and exiting the chamber, the O2-consumption and CO2-production can be calculated. In human subject studies, the metabolic chamber is replaced by a box enclosing only the head of the patient (sealed at the neck). Obviously, the stability regarding the environmental temperature is of overall importance. Equipment in support of acquiring genuine measurements include the following:

Diaferometer: An antiquated, yet notoriously accurate method for the simultaneous measurement of O2-consumption and CO2-production. It is, however, delicate and necessitates irksome calculation processes.

Paramagnetic gas analyzers (e.g. Oxymax): In consideration of the air perfusing throughout the interior of the chamber, the O2-consumption and CO2-production are continuously measured and the RQ is also computed.

These methods unavoidably necessitate a certain level of immobilization during measurements, particularly if the temperature of the specimen is to be recorded simultaneously. Naturally, this excludes the possibility of the parallel measurement of food intake (due to TEF and SDA, feeding would undoubtedly create a disturbance in the evaluation of MR.).

EXAMINATION OF METABOLIC RATE THROUGH SUSTAINED PERIODS

Method of doubly labeled water:

This particular method is effectively described in chapter 8. It allows the assessment of MR during everyday routine activity, over a span of three weeks. However, it cannot be used for assessing acute changes. The methods can be used for calculating the expectable energy need, including the personal allowance for people of various lifestyles or working conditions.

Principle: Following the consumption of D218O, the D2 is distributed in the total water space and emptied by the kidney, while one part of the 18O is distributed and emptied similarly, yet another portion is incorporated into the bicarbonate-pool and released in CO2 (as C18O2) through respiration. Accordingly, the two isotopes are emptied at different speeds. In the course of the three weeks, throughout various body fluids, the decrease of 18O-content is faster than the decrease in D2 content, hence, the higher the metabolic rate (and CO2 production), the greater will be the difference.

FACTORS INFLUENCING THE METABOLIC RATE:

Physiological: biological age, sex, body surface, TEF and SDA

Obligatory enhancing factors: TEF and SDA

Facultative enhancing factors: activity, NEAT, DIT, CIT, heat, stress and emotional state

Pathological: fever, inflammation, trauma, surgery, hyperthyroidism, extreme stress/adrenaline,

GH, DM, drugs (amphetamine) and, the recovery from catabolic states/diseases

A7.2. INVESTIGATIVE METHODS OF THERMOREGULATION

It is necessary, however, not sufficient, to collect data on the core temperature (Tc). Apart from this, information is required regarding the MR (see above) and on heat loss.

OBSERVATION OF HEAT LOSS

Either the evaporative heat loss can be assessed, through the rate of perspiration (sweat), respiratory rate in panting species, or the radiant heat loss based upon skin vasomotor changes (it is indicated by skin temperature /Ts/ which originates from the core, e.g. tail skin in the rat, ear skin in the rabbit, and their relationship with the ambient temperature /Ta/). The heat loss tendency may be assessed by a “heat loss index” (HLI), between values of 0 and 1:

HLI = (Ts - Ta) / (TC - Ta)

Values near 0 refer to vasoconstriction (heat conservation), and those near 1 refer to vasodilatation (and heat loss). Originally, HLI was abbreviated in the form as CVMI meaning, Cutaneous Vasomotor Index).

MEASUREMENT OF TEMPERATURE

Thermometers:

Values of core temperature measured in a given point of time provide only limited information in reference to changes in the regulation of body temperature. The mercury thermometers used in former medical practice were appropriate, however, only in the case of a fever. Contact thermometers measure in their own temperature, and, what is, due to their negligible heat capacity, equal with the contacted greater heat capacity system. Temperatures exceeding 41 ⁰C or below 36 ⁰C can also be measured with contact type tympanic thermometers.

Thermocouples:

Welding to different metals (e.g. copper-constantan) results in electric voltage, which is dependent upon the temperature of the welding point. While these thermometers can be used for measuring core temperature, the thermocouples can be fixed in the core or positioned upon the body surface, thereby permitting the long-term measurement of temperature of the given point. However, the investigative period is limited, of which, a certain immobilization is necessary. The recording apparatus cannot be too far from the measured point. However, in short-time measurements, thermocouples are excellent, and these are also preferred in the determination of HLI.

Transmitter systems (e.g. MiniMitter):

Implantable (at any part of the body, primarily i.p.) transmitters continuously provide temperature-related radio frequency signals to an antenna nearby, and this converts the signals electronically into temperature signals. The data can be fixed and later resumed. The method allows continuous (lasting up to several months) measurement of the temperature in freely moving animals. It is also used among wild animals (antelope, bison, kangaroo, etc.), freely moving within their natural environment. It can be used for analyzing circadian temperature rhythms. If and when combined with an implanted osmotic pump, the effects of chronic infusion of various drugs can be studied.

Similar transmitters can be used for recording the general activity and also the heart rate (it may be an indicator of the actual metabolic rate).

A7.3. INVESTIGATION OF FOOD INTAKE

MEASUREMENT OF THE CONSUMED FOOD

In human subjects, the measurement of consumed food and the recording of the data is achieved by conventional methods, however, only longer periods are generally investigated.

In animal studies, the measurement of solid food consumption is inaccurate or can only be accomplished over a longer period of time. In a short period of time, such as 1-2 hours in length, a rise in body weight may correspond in parallel, to a short-term enhancement of food intake (also considering the loss by excrement). To avoid these problems, feeding may be measured in animals habituated to the hourly/daily consumption of fluid mixture.

Feed scale: In this system, the weight of a container (containing powderized food) is measured continuously, and the animals are allowed to freely and randomly eat from the container. The data on the container weight can be stored and processed electronically. Circadian rhythms of food intake can be analyzed including chronic drug effects or the feeding habits in the case of alterations of food composition. In the long run, the acquired data has to be combined with daily body weight measurements.

STUDYING FEEDING BEHAVIOR

Formerly, the spontaneous feeding behavior (frequency of feeding, duration of feeding bouts, etc.) was analyzed by traditional observation. Recently, photocell systems are applied, and are described as an interruption of a light beam, which is recorded, the data stored and electronically processed.

There are several methods for the analysis of the often referred to, operant-behavior, the animals must act, and do something, such as crossing an electrical field, in order to reach the food source. This apparatus can be used to investigate various complex behavioral patterns involving food intake.

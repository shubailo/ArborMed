A6. PRINCIPLES OF HUMAN NUTRITION

The optimal nutrition that meets the needs of the organism is complex and secures energy utilization in good general conditions (ch. 8.1.1.) and, on the long run, the standard body weight and composition. There are general principles regarding the ideal nutrient intake, but in the everyday practice, nutrition must always be adjusted to the actual state, habitus and activity of the person.

Basic nutrients are: proteins, carbohydrates, fats, water, vitamins, minerals. Those substances with daily need exceeding 100 mg are regarded macronutrients (e.g. carbohydrates, fats, proteins, Ca, Na, K, according to some also vitamin C), while from micronutrients (minerals, vitamins) less is enough. As regards the composition of energy-supplying nutrients, it seems optimal if the daily energy intake is covered from carbohydrates by 55-75%, from fats by maximum 15-30%, and from proteins by 10-15%, distributed evenly for 4-5 meals per day (Table 8.1.).

A6.1. NUTRITIONAL RECOMMENDATIONS

A6.1.1. CALORIE-CONTAINING MACRONUTRIENTS

CARBOHYDRATES

Carbohydrates function mainly as energy sources. They may have complex forms (polysaccharides, e.g. starch, glycogen) or may be mono- vs. di-saccharides (e.g. glucose, fructose vs. saccharose, lactose). The carbohydrate-containing nutrients can be classified by the glycemic index (GI), which describes the effect of the given food to elevate blood glucose as compared with glucose intake (for which the GI = 100). The GI depends on the variety of carbohydrate (Fig. 9.25.), the composition of food, and from the simultaneously consumed other nutrients. Consumption of low GI food is advised: vegetables, whole grain wheat. Quickly absorbed monosaccharides should be avoided, since they are followed by fast and large deviations in the level of glucose and in enhanced insulin secretion, they cause a tendency for storing and obesity, and acutely the insulin induces hunger and further calorie intake (the glucose not utilized for immediate energy production is stored in the form of glykogen or triglyceride).

The nutritional fibers form a special group of carbohydrates, they are also called ballast material. Such are the cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin and various derivatives of pectin. These are natural component of the plant skeleton, the human digestive enzymes can at most partially decompose them, therefore they do not carry utilizable energy. Much is found in corn-grains, vegetables and fruits (cabbage, carrots, pea family, corn, apple, etc.). Due to their most important features (water-binding, cation-exchanging, adsorptive) – in case of food containing enough fibers – the volume of feces increases and the transit-time get longer. Higher fiber content allows protracted carbohydrate absorption and more balanced regulation of blood glucose. Gases from the fermentation increase the frequency of intestinal movements, and many (some toxic) substances may be excreted by binding to these fibers (e.g. bile acids), therefore they have a possible role in the prevention of colorectal tumors. The advised daily amount is 25-30 g.

In food industry often glucose is used to increase palatability and thereby the consumed amount. The foodstuff rich in added (purified) sugar belongs to the empty calories, which are rich in fat and/or sugar content, but poor in micronutrients (junk-food), their regular and large consumption is a risk factor of obesity. In contrast, out of the corn products, the foods made of flour of whole grain and corn-flakes contain a lot of fibers, vitamins and minerals, they are more satiating the food made of white flour, they decrease the risk of obesity, 2DM, cardiovascular and cancer diseases. Their use (cornflakes of rye) is also advised to decrease cholesterine content. Upon increased consumption of wheat-bran the absorption of bivalent cations was shown to decrease (its fitate content binds Fe, Zn, Mg, Ca, but also vitamin D and folic acid). For sweetening, honey is advised instead of sugar, because it contains also many additional nutrients.

LIPIDS

Fats and oils are mainly energy suppliers, but also enable people to take up fat-soluble vitamins, they incorporate into basic elements of membranes and nerves, they may have thermoregulatory function and provide mechanical defense (e.g. around the eye, kidney). The biological value of triglycerides is given by the type of fatty acid they contain. The unsaturated fatty acids are preferred over certain saturated ones (e.g. palmitic acid, most of which increase the level of cholesterol and the risk of cardiovascular disease), the consumed lipids should contain saturated:mono-unsaturated:poly-unsutrated (PUFA) fatty acids in a ratio of 1:1.2:0.8. PUFA-s are essential, the human body is unable to produce them, in their absence eczema-like skin lesions, anemia, thrombocytopenia develop, infections occur, the wound-healing is poor, the prostaglandin-synthesis decreases. Members of the group are fatty acids like the (-6 (e.g. linolic acid) and the (-3 (e.g. α-linolenic acid, eicosapentaenic acid) fatty acids. Denomination occurs according to the location of the double binds. The (-6 fatty acids are present in plant-oils (sunflower, olive, corn, wheat-bud), they do not elevate the cholesterol level. The main sources of (-3 fatty acids are linseed, rape-seed, soybean, and see-fish, they have many physiological actions (influence the gene transcription, decrease the cholesterol-synthesis and -level, vasodilators by PGE3 production, inhibitors of coagulation and platelet-adhesion, -aggregation) – the produced TXA3 is ineffective. The advised daily PUFA consumption is 6-10% of the energy intake, and a ratio of 4-5:1 for the (-6:(-3 fatty acids is advantageous from the aspect of prevention atherosclerosis and cancer.

The trans fatty acids are natural constituents of fats and oils, which are present in milk products and in the meat of ruminants, but mainly in hydrogenized plant oils, e.g. margarines contain them. They are to be avoided, since they elevate the total- and the LDL-cholesterol and suppress the HDL level, and according to some data they enhance insulin resistance.

.

The cholesterol, which is necessary in membranes, in production of hormones and bile acids, may be synthesized in the cells, or it is taken up typically by consuming fats of animal origin. The still acceptable upper limit of consumption is 300 mg/day (an equivalent of about 1 egg-yolk). In practice the consumption is much higher – an English breakfast contains several times more cholesterol. Since high cholesterol level inhibits the key enzyme of cholesterol synthesis, the advised decrease of cholesterol intake in itself is less effective in decreasing the cholesterol level, the additional increase of PUFA and vegetable fibers is also important.

PROTEINS

Regular protein intake is necessary for continuous generation of cells and tissues. and composition. These are usually substances of animal origin (e.g. milk and its products, meat, eggs), but not all animal proteins are complete (e.g. gelatine, protamine). Complete protein sources may be vegetables as well e.g. the gluteline of corn, glutenine of wheat, soybean. Soy contains trypsin-inhibitor, which inhibits protein digestion, but can be inactivated by heat. (However, the goitrogenic effect of soy cannot be eliminated by heat.) Animal proteins are more favorable not only because of their amino acid composition, but their biological availability is better (85-90%) than that of vegetable proteins (ca. 75%). The higher fiber content of vegetables may also inhibit biological availability. In the absence of certain amino acids there is no N-balance, but addition of this amino acid (or incomplete protein, containing it) may re-establish N-balance. This is called completion. It is advised that the completing proteins are consumed in the same feed, e.g. combinations of cereals+potatoes, cereals+peas, peas+oily seeds. In South-America corn+bean is the preferred way of completion. In animal experiments addition of greater amounts of non-essential amino acids to otherwise complete diet led to deficiency of essential amino acids (= amino acid imbalance due to concurrency in limited absorption).

A6.1.2. NON-CALORIE-CONTAINING MACRONUTRIENTS

Macronutrients not serving energy are Na and Ca. For details of Ca-metabolism see ch. 10.6. In Hungary the Na-intake exceeds the advised daily amount (< 2-5 g/nap) by 5-times, what is an important factor in the development of essential hypertension (ch. 2.4.2.2.), while the K-intake, which is mainly vegetable origin, lags behind the needed 2-3 g/day. Reducing salt intake is difficult, since the commercial foods already contain about ⅔ of the daily NaCl need (cheap, color- and taste-improving, preserving, water-binding features – the consumers may become used to it). The rest of the excess salt is due to feeding habits. The natural salt content of raw food material is small, but during the industrial/household food preparation it increases to significant levels. In special situations the Na-intake is still important, without this hyponatremic disorders may develop.

A6.2. VITAMINS

A.6.2.1. GENERAL DATA ABOUT VITAMINS

Definition:

Originally certain biologically active amines (”vital amines”) were called vitamins because the human body requires them in minute quantities, yet is unable to produce them. Therefore their regular dietary intake is essential for human survival and health maintenance. Today, in chemical terms we would rather define vitamins as small organic molecules. Some of them are lege artis coenzymes, others influence metabolic rate through modification of enzyme functions, some of them enhance or decrease the nuclear transcription of certain genes (vitamin A and D), or capture free radicals (vitamin C, E).

In the lack of proper vitamin intake normal plasma levels are maintained Their storage is limited to muscles developing during physical exercise. As for the functions and needs of proteins, see ch. 9.1.1.

„Complete” or full value are those protein sources, which secure N-balance with the smallest amount, and which contain essential amino acids in appropriate quantity from existing internal stores until they also become depleted. Then plasma levels drop under a certain threshold level and specific symptoms of deficiency appear. The appearance of these deficiency symptoms may be prevented or cured by the intake of the Minimum Daily Requirement (MDR). This value was determined by extrapolation of experimental data gained from animals, or simply from the mean daily intake of symptom-free “healthy” general population. Vitamin requirements may show individual differences or vary under specific circumstances. During the recruitment in World War II a different value, the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) was created in the United States by enhancing the MDR (2x Standard Deviation was added to MDR, or in other cases MDR was multiplied by 2 or 3). Since then official RDA values have been modified several times (not always upwards).

Discovery of the preventive role of certain vitamins (e.g. antioxidants) in chronic degenerative diseases lead numerous researchers in the field of nutrition to promote a revision of the RDA concept. In their opinion RDA is sufficient to prevent the development of specific symptoms of deficiency, but ingesting “just enough” vitamins for many-many years we loose the possible cumulative preventive benefit of e.g. vitamins A, C and E in the prevention of carcinogenesis or atherosclerosis and in postponing aging.

Classification:

lipid soluble: vitamin A, D, E, K (substantial reserves/stores, except vitamin K.)

water soluble: vitamin B1, B2, B6, niacin, folic acid, vitamin B12, C, panthotenic acid, biotin (minimal or no reserves/stores, except for B12.)

According to the severity of vitamin deficiency, hypo- and avitaminosis can be differentiated, the complex deficiency is named polyhypovitaminosis. The excess is hypervitaminosis.

Specific conditions often associated with vitamin deficiencies:

Prolonged starvation (partial starvation is frequently leads to multiple vitamin deficiency - complex hypovitaminosis).

Diet (radical slimming diet, special diets, anorexia)

Drug treatment that interferes with vitamins, or other causes of increased requirement (rapid growth, pregnancy)

Excessive alcohol intake

Chronic diseases (case history is very important! e.g. gastrointestinal disorders: malabsorption or diarrhea leads to increased sequestration of vitamins.)

Causes of vitamin deficiency:

Insufficient intake (e.g. elderly people living on their own; starving people of poor countries; eradication of vitamin K producing normal enteric bacteria – they present a specific external source; or other enteric bacteria might brake down intrinsic factor essential for physiological vitamin B12 absorption; parasites might consume vitamins; cooking or prolonged storage of nutrients promote oxidation of vitamin C; in corn acetyl-piridine binds niacin that is unavailable without proper lysis.)

Malabsorption (e.g. in lipid soluble vitamin absorption: lack of bile acids is much more important than pancreatic lipase deficiency.)

Increased requirement (rapid growth; pregnancy; alcoholism; patients suffering from chronic diseases; diet rich in carbohydrates increases vitamin B1 requirement; diet poor in Trp enhances the need for niacin, because Trp is a precursor for niacin synthesis; millet rich in Leu contributes to Trp and niacin deficiency through amino acid imbalance.)

Intake of antagonists (e.g. isoniazid and metabolites of aminopterines antagonize niacin, folic acid and vitamin B6; there is antagonism between coumarines and vitamin K; alcohol and vitamins B1, B6 and folate; spasmolytic drugs and vitamins D, K and folic acid; cholestyramine and lipid soluble vitamins; while thiaminase of raw fish and certain intestinal bacteria may metabolize vitamin B1)

Chronic diseases

A6.2.2. DIAGNOSTICS OF VITAMIN DEFICIENCIES

In subclinical vitamin deficiencies (when a mere suspicion arises due to general symptoms of vitamin deficiency, like weakness, apathy)

- determination of decreased serum levels or urinary excretion

- measurement of decreased cellular or tissue levels, contents in cellular or tissue extracts e.g. red blood cell or leukocyte extract

- functional examination based on specific activity:

Determination of initial apoenzyme activity followed by vitamin (coenzyme) supplementation. A higher-than-usual rate of increase in activity proves prior deficiency (e.g. increase in red blood cell transketolase activity after addition of thiamine pyrophosphate. Similarly, GOT, GPT activity of a red blood cell extract increases considerably following pyridoxal-phosphate supplement).

In case if a vitamin is necessary for the synthesis of a certain product, measurement of the amount or activity of this product is useful (e.g. in suspect vitamin K deficiency prothrombin-time is determined, or in suspect vitamin-A deficiency the dark vision is examined /this is the weakest light intensity the patient is able to perceive/).

A6.2.3. HYPERVITAMINOSES

Excess vitamin intake (oral or intravenous) may lead to hypervitaminosis especially in case of lipid soluble vitamins that are easy to store and problematic to excrete. Among them, vitamin A (stored in the liver), vitamin D (has reserves in liver and adipose tissue), and vitamin E (accumulates in adipose tissue) may be prevalent. Vitamin K does not have extensive reserves! Water soluble vitamins are not stored in general, with the exception of vitamin B12, that has reserves large enough in the liver to last for as much as 3-6 years. Nevertheless water soluble vitamins may also be overdosed in case of excessive intake (e.g. vitamin B6 and C).

A6.2.4. LIPID SOLUBLE VITAMINS

A6.2.4.1. VITAMIN-A (RETINOL, AXEROPHTOL)

FUNCTIONS:

Vision:

In the retina vitamin A is taken up in form of all-trans retinol, then transformed into 11-cis-retinal, which binds to the amino group of a species specific opsin protein forming rhodopsin (visual purple) in the rods and iodopsin in cones. When rhodopsin is hit by a photon, the complex disassembles, all-trans retinal is produced, the conformation of opsin changes and through G-proteins it activates phosphodiesterase. This activated phosphodiesterase brakes down cyclic nucleotides (cGMP), consequently the Na-channels are closed, and the consequent change of membrane-potential provides the electrical signal for vision. [Unlike other neurons, retinal cells are characterised by a much higher resting potential (-30 mV) and they react to stimuli by hyperpolarisation(!) to -60 mV – this provides an electrical signal]. All-trans retinal must be re-transformed into 11-cis configuration before it is associated with opsin again. Rods are responsible for photo-sensitivity.

Chromatopsy (colour-vision) suffers a functional deficiency only in very severe vitamin-A deficiency, because cones are much more stable than rods. According to various opsins the cones may be sensitive for red, green or blue (trichromatic color vision). Color blindness due to lack of red or green component (dichromatic vision) exhibits X-chromosome-linked inheritance and affects ca. 1-5% of men, blue color blindness is rare (1:20.000) in both genders. In cases of incomplete color blindness, the sensitivity decreases to the affected color due to low opsin concentration, but vitamin A deficiency causes problems of color vision only in extremely severe cases.

(2) growth, reproduction

Vitamin-A plays an important role in growth, reproduction (spermatogenesis), embryonic development, maintenance of integrity in the skin and other epithelial or mesenchymal (bone, cartilaginous or connective tissue) structures.

In the past as a major mechanism retinol phosphate was supposed to transfer mannose, galactose or glucosamine to peptides or lipids producing glucoproteins or glucolipids which will become integrated in connective tissue, thus contributing to the health of the above structures.

Today it is known that the action of vitamin A is very similar to that of vitamin D. Lipid soluble vitamin-A binds to a serum-retinol-binding protein (sRBP), enters the cells through the membrane and is transferred by a cytoplasmic retinol-binding protein (cRBP) within the cell. Nuclear receptors of vitamin-A belong to the large steroid receptor superfamily, within which there is much structural homology. One of these nuclear receptors shows a real high affinity to vitamin-A and is called Retinoid A Receptor (RAR). Another of these receptors which binds predominantly 9-cis retinoic acid is named Retinoid X Receptor or Rexinoid Receptor (RXR). Both receptors (especially RXR) are prone to heterodimer formation, i.e. forming pairs with other (non-retinoid) steroid receptors. These heterodimers are able to bind to the hormone-responsive-element (HRE) sequences in the promoter region of hormone responsive genes and activating the transcription complex they initiate gene transcription (in other cases gene activation may be inhibited, e.g. some oncogenes are not activated in the presence of vitamin-A). Since all this process is based on the initial receptor-vitamin association, these receptors may be considered to be ligand-activated transcription factors. Today, it is evident how vitamin-A may influence so profoundly cellular physiology, embryonic development, cell growth and differentiation, tissue integrity and even the quality of the immune response.

(3) Scavenger

The vitamin A is also important in the immune system, and its precursor -carotene is a free radical scavenger.

DAILY REQUIREMENT: 700-900 μg/day A-vitamin (or 2.4 mg (-carotene, since not all is transformed to vitamin).

SOURCE:

Vitamin-A precursor -carotene (found in most colored fruits/vegetables: carrots, tomato, green pepper, pumpkin, asparagus, spinach, parsley, melons, apricots) is split into 2 vitamin-A molecules. ((- or -carotene yields only 1 molecule of vitamin-A.) Cod-liver oil, liver, milk or eggs are also good sources of vitamin-A. Retinol, retinal or retinoic acid are equally effective in general, but retinoic acid cannot form visual purple so it fails to ameliorate night blindness.

In dermatology synthetic versions (13-cis isotretinoin, aromatic-ring containing etretin) are used for treatment of hyperkeratotic states, acne, papilloma, epithelioma. Vitamin-A has presumed role in the prevention of epithelial carcinomas.

Vitamin-A is absorbed through the upper part of the intestines is transported in the lymphatic system in chylomicron particles and it is taken up from the blood by the liver as part of the chylomicron remnant. Within the liver Ito cells (stellatae) store it as an ester. Released from the liver vitamin A binds to retinal binding protein (RBP), some binds to transthyretin. Liver stores last for about 6 months.

CAUSES OF DEFICIENCY:

- Insufficient intake e.g. in regions where rice (poor in carotene) is the main source of calories or at the end of winter when intake of carotene-rich vegetables decrease.

- Enhanced requirement during gravidity, lactation or in fever.

- Deficient absorption in severe diarrhoea as part of general malabsorption. Pancreatic lipase is not necessary for vitamin-A absorption, bile on the other hand is essential. Biliary obstruction is of short duration so vitamin-A reserves are usually not depleted.

- Insufficient transport may occur in a-beta-lipoproteinaemia, protein-calorie malnutrition, deficient production of RBP.

- In liver cirrhosis lipid-storing Ito cells are transformed into myo-fibroblasts by tissue inflammation losing their capacity to store vitamin A.

SYMPTOMS OF HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

1.	Impaired regeneration of rhodopsin leads to night blindness (retinography detects a 600-700-times increase in perception threshold).

2.	Synthesis of glucoproteins and glucolipids might decrease by as much as 80-90%, epithelial structures are affected.

- The skin is hyperkeratotic and scaly, epithelial structures are injured. Consequently, tear production falls, glucoprotein content in the tear is decreased, the protective lacrimal layer on the eye balls is increasingly fragmented leading to xerophthalmia (= dry eye). This dry eye is easily infected, purulent conjunctivitis, keratomalacia, corneal ulcers might develop leading to real blindness.

In developing countries every year half millions of children die in intraorbital infections due to vitamin-A deficiency.

- Mucosa of the airways and uterus cervix turns into metaplastic epithel and starts to produce keratin (squamous metaplasia). Carcinoma of the airways may develop.

- Weight-gain stops, growth and bone formation is impaired, ratio of osteoblasts and osteoclasts is abnormal.

- Spermatogenesis decreases.

3.	There is an enhanced susceptibility for infections especially on mucosal surfaces. Airway and gastrointestinal infections are frequent, diarrhoea aggravates vitamin-A deficiency even further.

HYPERVITAMINOSIS:

- Acute toxicosis might develop following extremely increased intake (triple RDA = 3.6 mg/kg, i.e. daily 200-250 mg for an adult, or consumption of polar bear liver). Increased permeability of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) leads to increased intracranial pressure with consequent headache, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, confusion, epileptiform convulsions. Upon fundus (oculi) examination papilla edema may be observed. Finally, gomphosis (fibrous joints and bones) might lead even to death due to circulatory and respiratory failure.

- Symptoms of chronic iatrogenic overdose (>1.2 mg/kg) include headache (mild increase in intracranial pressure) fatigue, pruritus (itching), pain in limbs and joints, hepatomegaly, cirrhosis, loss of hair.

- In animal studies high doses of vitamin A were found to be teratogenic. Intrauterine abortion, stillbirth, birth defects, mental retardation was also observed. (For this reason in women administration of high doses of vitamin-A must be accompanied by oral contraceptive treatment, which is to be continued for at least 3 months after termination of vitamin-A). Oral contraceptives themselves induce hypervitaminosis by stimulation of RBP production (these contraceptives should be avoided before a planned pregnancy). Body lotions and cosmetics of unknown (often illegal) origin frequently contain high amounts of vitamin A. They should be avoided especially by pregnant women.

A diet extremely rich in carotenoids might lead to yellowing of the skin (carotinosis, most striking in the palms) or in rare cases to retinopathy. However, it is usually a benign condition.

A6.2.4.2. VITAMIN-D

FUNCTIONS:

Vitamin D is a member of the steroid hormone family. Its active form 1,25-dihydroxy-cholecalciferol (1,25-(OH)2D3) binds to intracellular (IC) receptors and in the nucleus it promotes the transcription of certain genes, the translation and protein synthesis (Ca-binding protein = CaBP).

.

1. Non-genomic effects:

-activates voltage-dependent Ca++ channels in the intestines, kidney and bone

-increases Ca++ release from intracellular stores, elevating IC Ca++ levels in the	mucosa

-stimulates phosphatidylinositol metabolism

2. Nuclear (steroid hormone type) effects:

-in the intestines Ca binding protein (CaBP) synthesis is stimulated and at the same	time Ca release is enhanced on the serosal side.

-in the bone the synthesis of osteocalcin, osteopontin and alkaline phosphatase are	promoted

As a net result vitamin D:

-increases serum Ca++ level directly through increased absorption of Ca++ and phosphate (PO4-) from the gut,

-enhances bone resorption, releasing Ca++ into the plasma (receptors are only found on the surface of immature osteoclasts),

-augments Ca++ reabsorption and PO4- excretion in the kidney (not necessarily a direct effect)

The consequent rise in serum Ca++ indirectly contributes to growth and to the incorporation of Ca++ into the bone structure.

Vitamin D receptors are also found in other organs (e.g. skin, pituitary, breasts, parathyroid, gonads, pancreatic cells, brain, skeletal muscles, monocytes, lymphocytes etc.) or in certain tumours (e.g. breast, melanoma, promyeloblastoma) the differentiation of which promotes the growth that would normally be inhibited by vitamin D. Osteoblasts have vitamin D receptors, too.

Vitamin D receptors may be coupled with receptors of other steroid hormones (e.g. RXR) forming a hetero-dimer, modifying (activating or suppressing) genomic effects of each other.

REQUIREMENT: 400 IU (international unit) = 10 (g. In seniors, double dose is advised, there are not enough data for administration of even higher doses. In case of increased need (in gestation, lactation, in newborns up to 2 years of age, limited sun exposure, unvaried diet poor in lipids) vitamin D supplement must be given.

SOURCES: Plant ergosterine derived ergocalciferol (D2) and cholecalciferol of animal origin (D3) are equally active members of the vitamin D family. In the diet cod-liver oil, milk and egg yolk are rich in vitamin D, but it can also be produced in the skin: 7-dehydro-cholesterol synthesized priorly in the Malpighi layer of the skin is broken up between carbon atom (C) 9 and C 10 by UV radiation producing first pre-D3 and then by isomerization vitamin D3.

In solar radiation the ratio of the UV range varies (clouds, air pollution and ozone layer may decrease, deficient ozone layer might increase its level) so it is important to have sufficient skin exposure. According to archeological findings vitamin D requirement and rickets appeared around 7-8000 years ago together with clothing covering most of the body surface.

Dietary vitamin D is absorbed through the intestines together with neutral lipids and is transported in chylomicrons mostly to stores in the adipose tissue, and to a lesser extent to the liver, where hepatocyte microsomal 25-hydroxylase (requires NADPH2, O2 and Mg++) turns it into 25-OH-D3. By specific transport protein it is transported to the kidney, where NADPH2-dependent parathormone (PTH) regulated mitochondrial 1--hydroxylase produces active 1,25-(OH) 2-D3. Parathormone and vitamin D are synergist hormones: PTH is responsible for the rapid increase of se Ca++ from bone, while vitamin D is responsible for the slow one by increasing absorption.

CAUSES OF DEFICIENCY:

1. Low intake

The ingested vitamin D might be insufficient (in preterm babies, vegetarians, people living on a fat-free diet) when combined with insufficient UV irradiation. In the elderly skin capacity to produce vitamin D might decrease to 25 %. Generally, a 30-min/day irradiation of the face and hands are considered to be enough. A mild whole-body skin erythema obtained in the beach (without sunburn!) would mean a gain of 10-25000 IU vitamin D. People of Northern countries (particularly in winter) are endangered, but vitamin D deficiency may also develop in Southern muslim countries in women continuously covered by chador or burka.

2. Defective absorption

- vitamin D absorption may be impaired in diseases with severe malabsorption or diarrhoea (e.g. gluten enteropathy)

- a diet containing too much grains or oxalate, insoluble Ca salts are formed, Ca can not be absorbed, vitamin D becomes inefficient

3. Absence of 25-hydroxylation: characteristic in liver failure

4. Disorder of 1-hydroxylation:

- chronic kidney failure

- familiar deficiency of 1-hydroxylase

- inhibition of 1-hydroxylase: in Cushing’s disease, steroid treatment, strontium toxicosis, hypophosphatemia

- hypoparathyreosis

5. Resistance of the target organ: vitamin D-resistant rickets (receptor deficiency)

HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

Vitamin D deficiency induced demineralization of bones appears in form of rickets in children, or in form of osteomalacia in adults.

-	Newborns suffering from rickets appear to be listless and irritable already in early stages of the disease, their muscles are usually weak and hypotonic. Muscle dystrophy and bone deformities develop due to hypocalcaemia and compensatory hyperparathyroidism. Insufficiently mineralised bones are not fully capable to support body weight, they bend forming knock-knee (genu valgum), or bowleg (genu varum). Fontanelles do not close in time, cranial sutures are not properly mineralised, they remain wide soft and mobile, calvaria are softened (craniotabes). Due to muscular traction the chest becomes bell-shaped (chicken breasted), at the attachement site of the diaphragm the indentation on the lower ribs is called Harrison’s groove. Delayed tooth development and caries are often seen.

-	Epiphyseal growth plates become wide, irregular and hazy as a consequence of compensatory overproduction of bone matrix. At the same time mineralization is severely inadequate. In the skull parietal flattening and frontal bossing leads to the development of caput quadratum, in the chest prominence of the costochondral junctions will form a rachitic rosary.

-	In osteomalacia bones are soft and they are easily distorted (deformation of the pelvis may lead to difficult delivery in females), minimal trauma may lead to fractures. These fractures show impaired healing. Bones are tender, (impairing physical activity), caries is severe and progressive.

HYPERVITAMINOSIS:

- Iatrogenic overdose is possible if daily intake is higher than 100.000 IU (overanxious parents might give too much vitamin D to their children, or technological problems in dairy production might result in a serious overdose if milk is fortified with vitamin D). Hypercalcemia normally decreases PTH secretion that leads to decreased rate of final activation of vitamin D (1--OH in kidney), but other, e.g. 24-hydroxylation might create hypercalcemia-inducing products. Therefore, vitamin D intoxication is possible.

- Consequences of hypercalcaemia include headache, nausea, vomiting, general fatigue, obstipation, metastatic calcification in the kidney (leading to chronic renal failure, polyuria, consequent EC hypovolaemia – exsiccosis – and polydipsia), in the vessels (resulting rigidity of the vessel walls leads to hypertension, that might lead to hypertonic encephalopathy or to increased intracranial pressure). Serum phosphate and cholesterol are elevated. Chronic hypercalcemia may lead to the development of peptic ulcer through greatly enhanced gastric acid secretion. In its risk group chronic hypercalcemia might promote the development of depression or even psychosis.

A6.2.4.3. VITAMIN E (TOCOPHEROL)

A whole group of compounds are called vitamin E including ---, and -tocopherol.

FUNCTION:

Earlier, vitamin E was called antisterility factor based on animal studies and human observations in the field of gynecology and urology. Today its antioxidant and free radical scavenger property is considered to be its outstanding physiological function.

Free Radicals (R•) are dangerously reactive usually oxygen containing compounds that contain electrons of unpaired spin (e.g. superoxide anion, hydroxyl radical, perhydroxyl radical etc.). One of their widespread abbreviated name, ROS, means Reactive Oxygen Species. This bigger group of compounds include hydrogen peroxide, peroxynitrite, singlet oxygen, perchlorate etc., that are not free radicals in a strict chemical sense, but real free radicals are spontaneously created from them.

These substances may be produced in physiological processes (e.g. in lysosomes of macrophages or neutrophil granulocytes, during oxidative activities of cytochrome P 450 or in the mitochondria) or they might enter the body as contaminating environmental toxins (paraquat, dioxin, CCl4) in food stuff, due to air-, water- or soil-contamination, or perhaps radioactive irradiation promote their production.

Free radicals might also have useful functions (like the NO•), but because of their excessive reactivity they are mostly damaging to cells or tissues.

VITAMIN E THEREFORE…

-	plays a preventive role in atherosclerosis, since it prevents the production of peroxy-fatty acids from poly-unsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), as a free radical scavenger it decreases the formation of modified lipoproteins (e.g. oxidized LDL) that can be taken up only by atherogenic scavenger LDL-receptors.

-	prevents ROS-induced DNA-, RNA-, chromosome-damage, that might serve as an initiating event of carcinogenesis, i.e. vitamin E plays a role in cancer prevention.

-	through inhibition of ROS-induced cross-bridge formation in membrane lipids, surface proteins, and/or polysaccharides, vitamin E serves as a membrane stabilizer ensuring proper red blood cell life span or maintaining normal conditions in the nervous system or in muscles.

-	free radicals might damage tertiary structure of proteins (e.g. via brake up of disulphide bridges), they might promote protein glycosylation (or glycation), modification of surface portions of membrane proteins, damage of enzyme proteins. Through prevention of these effects vitamin E might protect the structure of immune cells especially of T lymphocytes, contributing to immune competence.

-	Vitamin E supplementation is thought to be beneficial in conditions with enhanced exposure to ROS, e.g. in assisted hyperbaric O2 ventilation of immature (pre-term) infants, post-infarction reperfusion of the heart or brain, ageing (perhaps also in Alzheimer disease).

-	Increased muscle creatinine-kinase and liver xanthine-oxidase production in vitamin E deficient conditions indicate that vitamin E might have a role in the regulation of protein synthesis

REQUIREMENT:

10-30 mg/day, but 100-400 mg daily intake is suggested in cancer-prevention and in old patients.

Increased intake of other antioxidant vitamins might enhance protection, and they contribute to the recreation of each other. For example, vitamin E itself forms a tocopheryl• radical after neutralizing a free radical e.g. a membrane lipid-peroxide. This vitamin E radical can be reduced back to normal by vitamin C, forming an ascorbate radical. It spontaneously converts into dehydro-ascorbate, then reduced back by a dehydro-ascorbate-reductase enzyme to vitamin C with the aid of glutathione. (The oxidated glutathione is converted back by the help of NADPH). This is why vitamin C is considered a chain-breaking antioxidant, it stops the chain reaction initiated by free radicals.

In contrast, increased intake of trace elements (Zn, Mn, Cu, Fe, Se, etc.) needed for antioxidant enzyme activity does not yield such a benefit. While lipid soluble vitamin E and β-carotene protect primarily cell membranes, vitamin C due to its water solubility is mainly effective in the serum or the cytoplasm.

SOURCES:

Vitamin E mainly in form of -tocopherol, or in lesser ratio - and -tocopherol is found in the adipose tissue of marine animals, nuts and seeds (sunflower-seed, soybeans, peanuts, linseed sources of vegetable oil or butter) and in wheat germ, eggs, milk and oatmeal as well. After intestinal absorption lipoproteins transfer it to its stores in the adipose tissue, that are quite considerable. This is why vitamin E deficiency will not develop in an obstruction jaundice (bile duct obstruction), although bile acids are essential for vitamin E absorption. In such cases severe vitamin K deficiency develops prior to possible development of vitamin E deficiency.

Causes of deficiency:

1. In pre-term infants the tocopherol levels are low, because

-intrauterin vitamin E store formation is inadequate because only 20% of the			vitamin may pass the placenta,

-immature infants are usually not strong enough for proper breast feeding, so			dietary intake is also very low, especially in parenterally (intravenous 			nutrition) fed premature babies,

-intestinal absorption is inefficient,

-growth is very rapid presenting high requirement,

2. Malabsorption syndromes with fatty stool = steatorrhea (sprue, gluthen enteropathy, short bowel syndrome, enteritis, ulcerative colitis, etc.)

3. Protein-calorie malnutrition

-low vitamin E content in the diet

-transport lipoprotein levels decrease in the serum

-impaired absorption

4. Abetalipoproteinaemia (low concentration of transport molecules in the blood)

SYMPTOMS OF HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

-	In animal studies an artificial vitamin E deficiency lead to infertility, necrotizing muscle dystrophy, neural damage, liver lesions. In human vitamin E deficiency fetal damage, abortions, testicular damage were observed.

-	Red blood cell stability is decreased, leading to hemolytic anemia.

-	Involvement of the nervous system was indicated by slower neural conductance in thick myelinic fibers, lesions of the dorsal ascending spinal tract (sensation of vibration and proprioception are impaired), paralysis of oculomotor nerves, and damage of the basal ganglia of the brain stem.

-	Muscle weakness and muscle necrosis-induced creatinuria are characteristic, acidophil lipofuscin pigment (IC accumulation of peroxy-fatty acids may play a role in the formation) may be deposited in smooth muscles, in the reticulo-endothelial system (RES), and sometimes in the brain stem basal ganglia.

-	Increased risk for infections was also reported.

-	Vitamin E deficiency is thought to contribute to the development of retrolental fibroplasia (retinopathy of prematurity) following hyperbaric O2 ventilation in premature new borns with IRDS.

TOXICOSIS:

In premature infants, intravenous overdose of vitamin E may cause hepato-splenomegaly, obstruction jaundice, ascites, azotaemia. In adults it may antagonise intestinal vitamin K absorption leading to symptoms of vitamin K deficiency. (For this reason high doses of vitamin E potentiate the effect of vitamin K antagonist coumarine derivatives).

A6.2.4.4. VITAMIN K (PHYLLOQUINONE)

FUNCTIONS:

Certain proteins containing glutamate with an additional -carboxyl group (Gla), that enables them to bind Ca2+ require vitamin K for this post-translational -carboxylation. These include:

-	blood clotting factors # II, VII, IX and X

-	anticoagulant proteins C and S

-	osteocalcin, a bone matrix protein.

Vitamin K-epoxide, a metabolite produced during the process has to be reduced again with the help of NADH. This reduction is blocked by coumarine derivates.

REQUIREMENT:

Since it is produced by enteric bacteria recommendation of daily intake exists only for newborns. For mature newborns a single oral administration of 2 mg, for pre-term newborns a single intramuscular injection of 1 mg conaquinone is used for prophylaxis.

SOURCES:

Vitamin K belongs to the naphthoquinone family. Both vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) that is found in vegetables, and vitamin K2 (menaquinone) that is produced by bacteria of the colon (E. coli, B. fragilis) are possible sources of vitamin K, although the ileum is the best site for bile-assisted absorption of this vitamin.

CAUSES OF DEFICIENCY:

1. In infancy, because

-its transfer through the placenta is slow

-the gut is sterile, or the colonising bacteria can not produce vitamin K in 			sufficient quantities

-mother’s milk contains only low amounts of vitamin K

2. In adults

-impaired absorption: I./ obstruction jaundice: vitamin K is not stored in 				substantial quantities, deficiency might develop in a very short time (in a 			week!)   II./ In malabsorption syndromes, and diarrhea (sprue, gluten 			enteropathy, ulcerative colitis)

-application of broad spectrum antibiotics may wipe out enteric bacteria.

-in liver failure

-in coumarine treatment (competitive antagonist to vitamin K)

HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

Hemophilia-like bleeding disorder develops with hypoprothrombinemia (prothrombin-time may be normalized by parenteral vitamin K administration) and prolonged clotting time. Pre-term new borns are especially endangered in morbus hemorrhagicus neonatorum (vitamin K deficiency in infants), due to their immature vascular system severe bleeding disorders (e.g. hemorrhagic stroke) may develop. (As prevention, vitamin K is usually prescribed for not only the new born, but also for the mother during her pregnancy.)

HYPERVITAMINOSIS:

Iatrogenic overdose of artificial vitamin K may occur, leading to enhanced coagulation (increased risk for thrombosis), hemolysis due to rigid red blood cell (RBC) membranes, and decreased glycogen stores in liver and muscles. During physiological jaundice in the newborn vitamin K may compete with bilirubin for binding sites on albumin. In vitamin K overdose free lipid soluble bilirubin might pass through the blood brain barrier and damages cell membranes the central nervous system (CNS). In severe cases bilirubin might accumulate in the basal brain stem nuclei resulting in Kern-icterus (Kern = ganglion or nucleus in German).

A6.2.5. WATER SOLUBLE VITAMINS

A6.2.5.1. VITAMIN B1 (THIAMINE, ANEURINE)

FUNCTION:

In mammals thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) is an important co-enzyme:

In carbohydrate metabolism TPP is essential for:

-decarboxylation of -ketoacids, e.g. in aerobic glycolysis it is cofactor of pyruvate- dehydrogenase enzyme (transformation of pyruvate into acetyl-CoA), or in the Krebs (citrate) cycle it is coenzyme for -ketoglutarate-dehydrogenase (-ketoglutarate ( succinyl-CoA transformation)

-transketolase reaction, e.g. in the pentose-phosphate cycle catalyzing the transfer of a glucolaldehyde group from xylulose (5C) to D-ribose producing D-sedoheptulose (7C). Activity of this transketolase enzyme when measured in RBC-s shows a strong correlation with general vitamin B1 levels.

In CNS thiamine triphosphate plays a role in ion channel functions.

REQUIREMENT: 1.5-3 mg/day

SOURCES: Thiamine is found in vegetables, yeast, eggs, meat and in the outer coat of grains.

CAUSES OF DEFICIENCY:

-	Processing (milling, hulling) deprives grains of their outer coat and thiamine, so thiamine deficiency is endemic in regions where the primary source of nutrients is white (hulled) rice (Thailand, Burma, Vietnam). In white bread they use thiamine supplements to replace the lost vitamin B1.

-	In Europe or in North-America thiamine deficiency develops mostly in chronic alcoholism due to deficient intake and intestinal absorption of vitamins of the B group (complex deficiency). In the breakdown of alcohol the TPP has a role, therefore the need is higher in case of excessive alcohol intake. Birth defects of children born to an alcoholic mothers are largely attributed to vitamin B1 deficiency.

-	Increased requirements are expected in a diet rich in carbohydrates, in gravidity, lactation, during fever or hyperthyroidism, but a mixed healthy diet usually contains enough vitamin B1.

-	Antithiamines. Certain food items (some types of sea fish, some vegetables like Brussels sprouts, beet root, blueberries, black currant) or bacteria may contain thiaminase enzyme, that brakes vitamin B1 down.

In some instances Arctic foxes bred for their fur showed characteristic symptoms of vitamin B1 deficiency. These animals were fed by food containing thiaminase from raw fish. Cooking renders the enzyme harmless. According to certain observations, men may also develop vitamin B1 deficiency if thiaminase containing bacteria colonise the gastrointestinal (GI) tract.

HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

Since thiamine is essential in ATP producing processes of the carbohydrate metabolism, symptoms of energy deficiency will develop in the muscles and in the primarily glucose-dependent neural tissue. Beri-beri, the classical syndrome of vitamin B1 deficiency can be divided into two major types: “dry” beri-beri with neural symptoms, and “wet” beri-beri with cardiovascular failure (involving myocardium and smooth muscles of vessels). The former occurs mostly in the European-Caucasian type, the latter will rather appear in the Far-East. According to some observations in Europeans genetic deficiency of transketolase activity is the basis upon which alcoholic neuropathy develops.

Wet beri-beri

Smooth musles of peripheral arterioles cannot metabolise sufficient amounts of glucose to produce ATP, their tone and therefore the strength of vasoconstriction become impaired, generalised vasodilation develops. This fall in total peripheral resistance would mean the fall of blood pressure if an elevated cardiac output did not compensate. Increase in cardiac output involve increased heart rate (HR) and/or increased stroke volume. The former appears as tachycardia (this is not characteristic, but may happen transiently), the latter could involve various possible mechanisms: 1. A decreased TPR (low tone of arterioles = low afterload; due to energy deficiency) may help increasing the ejection fraction, but the high venous return would necessitate a higher contractility, too. This cannot be maintained for long, since the myocardium itself has energy deficiency, thus a higher end-diastolic volume is necessary. 2. Increased preload – diastolic dilation of the myocardium is not possible through active mechanisms due to energy deficiency, only through passive Starling mechanism leading to congestive dilative cardiomyopathy with high atrial and venous pressures.

Venous stagnation in the pulmonary circulation leads to dyspnea, in the systemic circulation leads to generalized edema (this is why this type of beri-beri is called wet), ascites and hepatomegaly. Cardiac output is elevated but still deficient due to the generalized vasodilation (very low afterload) – high output cardiac failure (ch. 2.1.4.5.). The clinical picture includes muscle atrophy, atonia of the stomach and the intestines combined with maldigestion. Cardiomyopathy is reversible upon vitamin B1 administration in time, the high output cardiac failure and edemas also disappear.

Dry beri-beri

Ascending myelin sheath and axon degeneration leading to polyneuritis and neuropathy in the periphery (starting from the feet and hands spreading towards the trunk) characterized by paresthesia, “burning feet” syndrome, muscle weakness, paralysis, cramps, reflex abnormalities. In the CNS Wernicke-Mann encephalopathy develops with headaches, fatigue, learning disability, in more severe cases confusion, dizziness, ataxia, ptosis, ophthalmoplegia, nystagmus and finally coma. In the brain encephalomalacia, purpurae can be observed. Korsakoff psychosis might also appear with defects in memory and confabulation (tales made up by the subject to fill in the gaps of memory). Peripheral symptoms are reversible upon thiamine administration, more severe encephalopathy can be ameliorated but it is partly irreversible.

Vitamin B1 has practically no reserves, overdose or hypervitaminosis (just as with other water soluble vitamins) does not occur.

A6.2.5.2. VITAMIN B2 (RIBOFLAVIN)

FUNCTION:

- Riboflavin is part of flavin mononucleotid (FMN, essential part of NADH-dehydrogenase) or flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD, essential part of pyruvate-dehydrogenase, succinate-dehydrogenase, acyl-CoA-dehydrogenase, xanthine-oxidase and aminoacid-oxidases). FAD and FMN also serve as co-enzymes to oxido-reductive processes in the final energy producing electron transport in pyruvate, fatty acid or amino acid metabolism.

-	Riboflavin also plays a role in the metabolism of other vitamins, e.g. folic acid (folate(formyl-tetrahydro-folate), B6 (pyridoxine phosphate(pyridoxal phosphate) and through B6 in the synthesis of niacin.

-	Antioxidant enzyme glutathione reductase is also B2-dependent.

-	Monoamine oxidase (MAO), essential in the metabolism of neurotransmitters also requires B2 for its activity.

REQUIREMENT: 2-6 mg/day

Sources: yeast, milk, eggs, fish, meat, grains and vegetables.

CAUSES OF DEFICIENCY:

-	This vitamin is ubiquitous, so vitamin B2 deficiency may occur only as part of a general hypovitaminosis (complex deficiency of group B) in malnutrition or chronic alcoholism.

-	Hypothyroidism and adrenal failure less vitamin B2 is activated.

-	During tricyclic antidepressant therapy of psychiatric disorders iatrogenic deficiency may develop.

HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

-	Inflammation of the gastrointestinal mucosa, stomatitis, glossitis, cheilosis, sore throat, seborrheic dermatitis might develop as a consequence of B2 deficiency.

-	A role in the production of RBC for vitamin B2 is suggested by the microcytic hypochromic anemia that develops in its deficiency.

It is not stored, there is no risk of overdose.

A6.2.5.3. VITAMIN B6 (PYRIDOXINE)

FUNCTION:

As pyridoxal phosphate or pyridoxamine phosphate, it is co-factor to many enzymes.

-	As co-factor of transaminases it is necessary for the first step of amino acid brake down and for the synthesis of non-essential amino acids.

Detecting the activity of GOT, GPT (AST, ALT) in RBC extracts after the addition of pyridoxal phosphate we may diagnose functional deficiency of vitamin B6.

-	It is part of the kinureninase and kinurenine transaminase enzymes that are necessary for the production of niacin from tryptophan (Trp). In vitamin B6 deficiency there is also a niacin deficiency, in the urine accumulation of kinurenic acid-xanthurenic acid metabolites may be observed, enhanced by surplus Trp intake.

-	Through decarboxylation of amino acids vitamin B6 also contributes to the production neurotransmitters e.g. dopamine, serine or inhibitory transmitter -amino-butyric acid (GABA).

-	As co-factor of transsulphurases, it is needed in the metabolism of sulphur containing amino acids.

-	Although it takes part in gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis (therefore raising blood glucose levels), deficiency will not lead to hypoglycemia.

-	Vitamin B6 binds to CD4 receptors of T lymphocytes, suggesting a role for vitamin B6 in immune processes. Similarly, it is able to bind to virus HIV-1, so its possible beneficial role in AIDS is being investigated.

-	Among other numerous functions, vitamin B6 is a steroid modulator, has a role in the biosynthesis of porphyrins and in the active transport of amino acids into the cells.

REQUIREMENT: 2mg/day.

SOURCES: milk, eggs, fish, meat, yeast, beans, banana, etc.

CAUSES OF DEFICIENCY:

-	In overheated baby formulas vitamin B6 is broken down, so vitamin B6 deficiency may lead to generalized cramps in newborns (due to lack of GABA, an inhibitory neurotransmitter).

-	In adults, chronic alcoholism, pregnancy and antagonist drugs (e.g. INH-isoniazid, used for the treatment of tuberculosis) might facilitate the development of deficiency. During treatment with antagonists, vitamin B6 must be given in high quantities.

HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

-	Convulsions of CNS origin mostly occur in babies, but abnormal EEG may develop in adults too.

-	Adults also develop microcytic hypochromic anemia and polyneuritis.

-	In experiments carried out in monkeys increased incidence of caries was observed in vitamin B6 deficiency. On the other hand, daily 3x3 mg vitamin supplementation for a year resulted in a 40% decrease in caries incidence in school children.

-	Symptoms may also include acute and chronic gingivitis, atherosclerosis, steatosis hepatis (fatty liver) and cirrhosis.

OVERDOSE:

In high doses and transiently vitamin B6 may be applied as “the most physiological” antiemetic drug, since it decreases the sensitivity of the vomiting centre (non-physiological rather a pharmacological effect!). It does not cause somnolence. It may be given in morning sickness or in sea-sickness, it may also be given to newborns or children. In sustained application high doses (several grams) lead to toxicosis involving CNS disorders and neuropathy.

Vitamin B6 has reserves for 2-6 weeks.

A6.2.5.4. NIACIN (NICOTINIC ACID)

FUNCTION:

Nicotinamide is part of NAD+ and NADP+, both of which are co-factors of oxidative-reductive processes. They serve as electron acceptors in dehydrogenation reactions. NADH transfers its electron to the mitochondrial electron transport-chain, NADPH supplies synthetic processes with H+.

REQUIREMENT: 20-25 mg/day

SOURCES:

Niacin is found in food e.g. in meat, milk, vegetables, eggs, yeast, etc. In a limited rate, niacin is also produced from Trp in the body (60 mg Trp ( 1 mg niacin). Increasing protein intake may reduce niacin requirements.

CAUSES OF DEFICIENCY:

A diet deficient in niacin in itself or in combination with low Trp and/or vitamin B6 intake leads to the development of symptoms. Pellagra, the specific disease caused by niacin deficiency used to be endemic in America and in certain regions of Europe during the XVIIIth century, and even in the beginning of the XXth century it was still quite prevalent in places where proteins of animal source were hardly consumed and the primary source of energy was maize (corn) containing niacin mostly in a bound form and also being poor in Trp.

Native Americans treated maize with slaked lime (alkali) liberating niacin from its bound form (Homini), but immigrants did not adopt this technique.

Pellagra may also develop as a result amino acid imbalance: pellagra is also endemic in such regions of India where millet is the main source of energy. Due to its high leucine (Leu) content Trp primarily enters into protein synthesis and hardly contributes to niacin formation. (A kind of amino-acid imbalance)

HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

Clinical symptoms of pellagra are characterised by the “3 D”-s: dermatitis, diarrhea and dementia (the 4th possible D is for death in case of a lethal outcome).

-	Skin surfaces exposed to sunlight develop photosensitivity, erythema and skin lesion that are hyperkeratotic, hyperpigmented, blistered and scaly. The name of the disease means raw, rough skin (pelle-agra = “skin of agricultural workers”). On the face symmetrical skin lesions resemble a butterfly, on the neck Cassal’s necklace appear.

-	Atrophy of the intestinal mucosa and gastrointestinal inflammation lead to diarrhea and vomiting. The clinical picture also includes stomatitis and glossitis (bright red tongue).

-	Neural symptoms might be mild (insomnia, headache, fatigue, slight impairment of memory) or severe: confusion, disorientation, hallucinations, manic-depression, mental deterioration and also apathy. In the background low 5-hydroxy-tryptamine levels (serotonin, a Trp-derived mediator in the CNS) may be found. Low urinary excretion of 5-hydroxy-indoleacetic acid in pellagra patients also shows serotonin deficiency.

HYPERVITAMINOSIS:

A serum cholesterol (se chol.) reducing effect (LDL-chol. (, HDL-chol. () is attributed to niacin, therefore it is prescribed in large doses. A dose of 1.5-3 g/day may ave side effects like erythema, hyperuricemia (high se uric acid levels), slight liver damage, with a tender liver.

A6.2.5.5. VITAMIN C (ASCORBIC ACID)

FUNCTION:

Ascorbic acid is a vitamin for only those species, in which the enzyme L-gulonolactone-oxydase is missing, for humans, primates, guinea pigs, bats, for many kinds of fish and birds and also for most arthropodes.

Albert Szentgyörgyi was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1937 for his work on the structure and role of vitamin C in cellular respiration. He was the first to extract vitamin C from adrenal glands in 1928 and later in larger quantities from vegetable green pepper (red paprika) of Szeged in 1931.

-	Vitamin C is an antioxidant, plays an important role in the defense against free radicals (R•) in water-soluble surroundings, indirectly also contributes to the protection of lipid-soluble membranes (see also vitamin E). It keeps iron in a ferrous form (Fe2+) that can be absorbed in the intestines (and may also enhance the mobilisation of Fe from the RES cells). This Fe2+ form is needed for the proper functioning of various enzymes. Vitamin C also keeps catecholamines and tetrahydro-folate (H4-folate) in a reduced state.

-	Ascorbic acid serves as a co-factor of enzymes Pro- and Lys-hydroxylase, that carry out the posttranslational hydroxylation of Pro and Lys residues within the polypeptide chain of collagen, before assembling the triple helix structure. These hydroxyl groups are essential for the cross-linking of the three polypeptide chains that will ensure the stability of collagen.

- Vitamin C also plays a role in steroid hydroxylation in the adrenal cortex and in norepinephrine synthesis in the adrenal medulla as co-factor of dopamine--hydroxylase. During stress besides cortisol and catecholamines, ascorbic acid is also released from the adrenal glands.

-	It is needed for the transformation of cholesterol into bile acids (involving hydroxylases that contain vitamin C-dependent cytochrome P 450), thus promoting the biliary excretion of cholesterol. This effect combined with being a free radical scavenger explains the role of vitamin C in the prevention of atherosclerosis.

-	The presence of vitamin C is essential for normal cell-mediated immunity. Immune cells (e.g. macrophages) maintain their high intracellular ascorbic acid levels (1-2 mM, several times higher than the plasma level of 0.03-0.15 mM) through active transport processes.

-	It is co-factor of para-hydroxy-phenyl-pyruvate-hydroxylase (involved in Tyr metabolism and the first step in serotonin synthesis). In vitamin C deficiency para-hydroxy-phenyl-pyruvate is excreted in the urine. In neurons ascorbic acid levels as high as 6-10 mM were observed.

-	Some studies suggest that vitamin C decreases malignant tumor formation, others appear to contradict them.

-	Under specific circumstances vitamin C may slow down the ageing process.

REQUIREMENT:

A daily intake of 10-20 mg/day may prevent the development of scurvy, but at present the official daily recommended intake is 70-100 mg/day. There is an ongoing debate concerning the exact amount. Acknowledged experts in nutrition (Szent-györgyi, Pauling) insist that daily intake of vitamin C should be above 400 mg for us, to be able to profit from long-term prevention potential (in carcinogenesis and atherosclerosis), despite the fact that kinetic studies showed 97% of the surplus vitamin C (above 100 mg/day intake) to be excreted swiftly through the urine and overdose is also possible (see later).

SOURCES:

Fresh, pickled (sour) or dried vegetables and fruits (rose-hip, currant, bramble, apple, spinach, broccoli, paprika, tomato, raw potato, asparagus, different types of lettuces, pickled cabbage (Sauerkraut), citrus fruits: orange, lemon, grape-fruit). Cooking (loss of 80%), preservation, storage, freezing (loss of 50%) greatly reduces vitamin C content, oxidation renders vitamin C inefficient. Oxidation is facilitated in presence of even a small amount of metal ions (iron, copper) producing ascorbate free radical besides other reactive oxygen species. In this form vitamin C becomes pro-oxidant, i.e. may have damaging effect on cells and DNA.

It has already been recognized by Szentgyörgyi that vitamin C requirement of guinea pigs receiving a small dietary supplement of green plants (besides the purified ascorbic acid) is greatly reduced. The vitamin C sparing compound turned out to be a flavonoid that was first named citrin by Szentgyörgyi. Later the same compound was renamed vitamin P (permeability vitamin) based on its protective effect on blood vessels and reducing vascular fragility. The exact structure has not been discovered yet but two compounds: quercetin and its glycoside – rutin – fulfill the criteria originally used for the description of vitamin P. (Rutascorbin®, a pharmaceutical product containing rutin besides vitamin C is available in Hungary). Although rutin has not been registered as a vitamin, several other members of the plant flavonoid family have been shown to have potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and/or tyrosine-kinase inhibitory and/or endocrine regulatory functions contributing to their anticancerous effect.

CAUSES OF DEFICIENCY:

- Deficient intake might occur during winter and spring among people on a monotonous diet (elderly!).

- Requirement increase during gravidity, through periods of rapid growth, in infections and fever.

HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

- In vitamin C deficiency decrease in the posttranslational lysyl-hydroxylation leads to impaired collagen stability, in severe vitamin C deficiency collagen synthesis is blocked and collagen content of some structures like capillary basement membrane become poor in collagen and consequently become fragile. These changes may explain one of the characteristic symptoms of scurvy the classical disease of vitamin C deficiency:

vascular purpura – small bleedings in the skin and in the mucosa. Later in more severe cases thin but widespread layers of hematoma might develop in the skin, in internal organs, joints and under the periosteum. Scurvy of the new born (Möller Barlow disease), involve large bleedings under the periosteum and painful deforming arthritis.

wound heeling is typically prolonged

parodontopathy, looseness of teeth, loss of teeth, prolonged heeling of bone fractures, according to some experts even osteoporosis may develop.

Scurvy used to be a characteristic disease of sailors on long sea voyages. Small bleedings (gingival) offered weak spots where infections could develop, prolonged wound healing increased the risk for bacterial contamination, poorly hydroxylated collagen was less resistant against the collagenase enzyme of bacteria so spreading of bacteria and toxins was enhanced. Finally, severe infections, gangrena and non-healing wounds lead to lethal sepsis.

Nowadays vitamin C deficiency appears in its milder forms, instead of scurvy we see spring exhaustion and muscle weakness:

- in vitamin-C deficiency immune functions are impaired.

- chronically low intake results in enhanced atherosclerosis since less cholesterol will be transformed into bile acids resulting in higher se cholesterol levels and also more LDL will be modified through oxidation

-	impairment of intestinal iron absorption may lead to microcytic, hypochromic anemia, that can be aggravated and complicated by low levels of active folic acid (shift towards macrocytic hyperchromic form of anemia)

OVERDOSE:

Daily intake of 0.5-1 g vitamin C for prevention of infections, atherosclerosis or ageing might contribute to the development of kidney stones (oxalate or urate) in people predisposed for kidney stone formation, or it may also cause gastrointestinal discomfort. If these high daily doses are taken in liquid form (mixed into a drink) instead of in cachet or in form of intestinosolvent dragee (a special pill dissolving only in the intestines, not in the stomach) its strong acidity may lead to erosions of tooth enamel.

A6.2.5.6. FOLIC ACID (VITAMIN B11)

FUNCTION:

Tetrahydrofolate (FH4), the active form of vitamin B11 is capable of transporting C1-moieties (methyl-, methylene-, methenyl-, formyl- or formimino-groups) to N5 or N10 sites, thus taking part in the following reactions:

-	synthesis of purine and pyrimidine (e.g. transformation of uracyl into thymine)

-	fixation of the formimino group

-	metabolism of amino acids:	Gly ( Ser

homocysteine ( Met

form-imino-glutamate (FIGLU) ( Glu

The latter is of diagnostic importance: in folic acid deficiency FIGLU content of the urine is elevated.

REQUIREMENT: 0.5 mg/day

Since folic acid requirement is enhanced through pregnancy, it is advisable to start taking in more vitamin even before conception (a daily 2x2 mg folic acid supplement is prescribed by the physician upon diagnosis of pregnancy) to prevent the neural tube defects (deficiency of closure: spina bifida = cleft spine) of the fetus. In smokers, a daily intake of 1 mg folic acid in excess is suggested to prevent the development of precancerous metaplasia of the respiratory mucosa, in women folic acid has been shown to have a role in the prevention of precancerous states of the cervix.

SOURCES: green plants (folium = green leave), fresh vegetables, liver, walnuts, chocolate.

CAUSES OF DEFICIENCY:

-	insufficient intake (e.g. cooking damages folic acid)

-	increased requirement in pregnancy, hyperthyroidism, hemolytic anemia quickly depletes its reserves (5-10 mg) in the liver. In alcoholics the stores are even lower.

-	intestinal absorption becomes deficient in diarrhea (e.g. in tropical sprue mucosal atrophy develops in the jejunum)

-	drug therapy antagonizing folic acid (e.g. methothrexate used in tumor therapy)

-	secondary folic acid deficiency occurs in vitamin B12 deficiency through a block of homocystein ( Met transformation, in which methyl-H4-folate regenerates into H4-folate. Lack of vitamin B12 leads to an accumulation of methyl-H4-folate that is incapable of transporting C1 groups (methyl-folate trap).

-	vitamin C deficiency may also lead to symptoms of folic acid deficiency because of its role in the dihydro-H4-folate ( H4-folate reaction.

HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

Due to its role in purine and pyrimidine synthesis folic acid deficiency will impair cell division.

-	In the bone marrow there is a normal production of hemoglobin. The number of available RBC decrease. So RBC-s become large containing more hemoglobin (megaloblastic erythropoiesis), irregular in shape (oval or spiky). Their life span becomes shortened, and many RBC-s are destroyed within the bone marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis). Since besides RBC, leukocytes and platelets are also involved in the ineffective erythropoiesis, in the periphery macrocytic, hyperchromic anemia (with hemolysis), leukocytopenia and thrombocytopenia (pan-cytopenia) can be observed. Hypersegmentation of leukocytes (“over-maturation”) may be an early sign. If iron deficiency also develops macroovalocytosis may be absent.

-	The gastrointestinal mucosa is also characterized by rapid cell turnover. In folic acid deficiency atrophy, secondary inflammation and bleedings of the intestinal mucosal villi may occur.

- In sustained folic acid deficiency dementia and psychiatric abnormalities may develop as a result of deficient methylation and hydroxylation of biogenic amines. Childhood hypovitaminosis can cause mental retardation.

A6.2.5.6. VITAMIN B12 (CYANOCOBALAMINE)

FUNCTION:

Vitamin B12 serves as co-enzyme in two reactions:

-	It catalyzes the propionyl-CoA ( succinyl-CoA reaction, which is an intermediate step (link) between carbohydrate and fat metabolism. When this step is blocked, the accumulating short-chain fatty acid fragments build into myelin and cause damage.

-	Through its role in the homocystein ( Met transformation cyanocobalamine helps the regeneration of H4-folate from methylene-H4-folate. Without this process, methylene- H4-folate produced in different reactions of the body irreversibly turns into 5-methyl- H4-folate (“methyl-folate trap”). Purine and pyrimidine synthesis are indirectly damaged and lack of thimidine will lead to deficient DNA production.

REQUIREMENT: 1-2.5 g/day, but stores in the liver (2-4 mg) last for 3-6 years.

SOURCES:

All animals (and humans) utilise vitamin B12 of bacterial origin. Humans acquire this vitamin mostly from animal sources (meat, liver, egg, milk). For its intestinal absorption first of all a glucoprotein R is needed that escorts vitamin B12 to the duodenum. After their dissociation the vitamin is adsorbed by the intrinsic factor (IF) produced by the parietal cells of the gastric mucosa, providing protection against oxidation and bacterial utilization. The IF-B12 complex then binds to a mucosal receptor in the ileum, and being released from the IF it is absorbed by the mucosal cell. Within the circulation B12 is transported by its transport protein transcobalamine to the liver where it is stored.

Intrinsic factor production shows a strong correlation with gastric acid production. Without IF vitamin B12 absorption is negligible. Old therapies were based on this negligible non-specific diffusion: large quantities (several kilograms) of minced raw liver was fed to these patients. Today parenteral vitamin B12 injections are administered in deficiency states. Such treatment quickly normalizes the RBC formation: at the beginning “reticulocyte crisis” may be observed (= remains of nuclei in the freshly produced RBC-s), later the fast Fe-utilization may lead to iron-deficiency anemia.

CAUSES OF DEFICIENCY:

1. Insufficient intake occurs only in strict vegetarians (if they refuse to eat eggs and milk products too), even then the symptoms develop very late, after the depletion of stores in the liver.

2. Deficient absorption:

-	IF deficiency may be congenital or acquired (autoimmune atrophic gastritis that may be combined with diabetes, Addison’s disease, Hashimoto thyreoiditis, hypoparathyroidism – as part of the polyglandular autoimmune endocrine deficiency – autoantibodies may inhibit the IF- B12 adsorption or their binding to their receptor in the ileum). When atrophic gastric mucosa is unable to produce either gastric acid or IF, we speak about histamine-refractory achlorhydria. (In the test nowadays instead of histamine pentagastrin is used.) In case of total gastrectomy (in tumour or ulcer therapy) IF deficiency also has to be considered.

-	Resection or inflammations of the ileum (e.g. tropical sprue, gluthen enteropathy, Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis), particularly those affecting the terminal ileum.

-	Parasites may consume the vitamin before absorption (e.g. Diphyllobothrium latum is wide spread in fish in Scandinavia); bacteria from the colon abnormally colonising the ileum might also brake up the IF-B12 complex.

Schilling test is used for the clinical examination of B12 absorption: The stores in the liver are filled with a prior injection of unlabelled vitamin B12. Then labelled (radioactive) vitamin B12 is given per os and then urinary excretion of this labelled vitamin is measured. If the urine contains less than a certain percentage of the ingested labelled vitamin the test is repeated with added IF.

3. Disorders of storage in liver cirrhosis.

HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

- Secondary pernicious anemia (due to folic acid deficiency) is the most characteristic symptom, with ineffective erythropoiesis in the bone marrow and mild hemolysis in the periphery due to the abnormal size and shape of RBC. The mild jauindice may be explained partly by the hemolysis, or by shunt-hyperbilirubinaemia (ch. 7.6.1.3.).

-	Due to the high hemoglobin content and relatively high O2-transport capacity of individual RBC, very low RBC counts (1 T/l compared to the 3 T/l in iron deficiency anemia) may be survived. Hematocrit may be as low as 10-15%. This anemia may be ameliorated by folic acid administration.

-	Abnormalities in myelin production may appear as peripheral paresthesia, muscle weakness. Demyelinization of the dorsal ascending spinal tracts or lateral spinal tracts will lead to funicular myelosis with severe ataxia. Similar demyelinization may also develop in the brain. Neurological symptoms may also occur with normal hematocrit. Chronically sustained neurological symptoms usually become irreversible. While folic acid administration is useful in treating anemia, it may worsen the neurological consequences.

-	Atrophy of the gastrointestinal mucosa develops, Hunter’s glossitis is a characteristic symptom of vitamin B12 deficiency, but gastric atrophy may also develop (it may be a cause, and it may also be a consequence).

A6.2.5.7. PANTOTHENIC ACID (B5 VITAMIN)

ROLE:

It is a component of CoA, thus, it contributes in the activation of acetic acid and fatty acids of longer chain, i.e. in the intermediary metabolism of fats, proteins and carbohydrates as well as in the cholesterol- and steroid-metabolism.

HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

Due to its common occurrence, typical deficiency symptoms are rather rare. In animal studies its deficiency is assumed to play a role in the early greying and loss of hair, feather, in the stop of growing, in necrosis of the adrenal gland, while in humans the defect may be a causative factor in the burning feeling of the feet in partially starving persons, in other cases in the development of hypertension.

A6.2.5.7. BIOTIN (H-VITAMIN, B7-VITAMIN)

ROLE:

As a prosthetic group, it participates in CO2-fixation and -transmission, has a role in the function of acetyl-CoA-carboxylase, propionyl-CoA-carboxylase and pyruvate-carboxylase as well as in that of transcarboxylases, i.e. has a role both in gluconeogenesis and fatty acid synthesis.

HYPOVITAMINOSIS:

Biotin in the nature is rather common, and bacteria can also synthesize it. However, the avidin of raw egg-white binds it, preventing its absorption. Deficiency symptoms occur in persons consuming larger amounts of raw egg, and also after lasting treatment with sulfonamides that destroy the intestinal bacterial flora. In humans, dermatitis and anemia may develop as deficiency symptoms.

A6.3. TRACE ELEMENTS

IRON (Fe)

It is an essential constituent of hemoglobin (Hb), myoglobin, cytochromes and certain enzymes e.g. catalase, Pro-, Lys-hydroxylase.

Although the body is able to use again iron released from demolition of Hb and other forms, some loss still occurs, this is to be replenished. The daily iron loss is about 1 mg by desquamating intestinal cells and some skin cells. Women also lose iron by menstruation, their daily iron need is therefore 3 mg. Iron can be absorbed in the small bowel in Fe2+ (ferro) form. The iron of vegetable origin is mainly in ferri (Fe3+) form, which is to be transformed to ferro form with gastric acid and vitamin C. About 10% is absorbed of the iron derived from vegetables and milk, while for iron of animal origin this may reach 25%. Accordingly, the required daily intake is 10-30 mg, in children, pregnant and lactating women even 40 mg. The normal se-Fe is 7-27 mol/l.

After absorption, in the blood Fe3+ (transport form) is carried by transferrin. In tissues it is stores as ferritin or pathologically deposited as hemosiderin. If the stored Fe decreases, the intestinal absorption rate may increase from 10 to even 16%. The rise is not specific for iron: in Fe-deficiency the absorption of other metals, e.g. Pb also increases, what is not favorable particularly due to Pb-contamination of urban air.

Frequent causes of Fe-deficiency:

- deficient intake, e.g. strict vegetarianism, diet, starvation

- poor absorption, e.g. gastric acid deficiency, vitamin C-deficiency, chronic diarrhea, malabsorptions (e.g. celiac syndrome)

- enhanced need (childhood, puberty /also boys during fast growth and muscle cell formation/, pregnancy, lactation, uremic or other hemolysis)

- enhanced loss, e.g. repeatedly strong menstrual bleeding, gastrointestinal (occult) bleedings, chronic diarrhea (loss of epithelial cells)

- chronic inflammatory diseases: Upon IL-1 the neutrophil granulocytes produce lactoferrin, which binds iron. The lactoferrin-Fe complex is phagocyted by macrophages and cannot release the iron. This is part of the natural defense reaction, since the bacteria, particularly at high (febrile) temperatures, need more iron for multiplication. Thus, it is advantageous for the host to decrease the se-Fe during infection.

Fe-deficiency is the most widespread deficiency disease in the world, it occurs frequently even in developed countries!

Consequences of Fe deficiency:

- at the beginning, the Fe-binding capacity slightly increases (> 360 g/dl) and decreases the serum ferritin level (< 20 g/L). In moderately severe Fe deficiency, besides further increasing Fe-binding capacity and low se-ferritin, the serum-Fe-level falls below 7 mol/liter, the sideroblast ratio of bone marrow is below 10%, the protoporphyrin content of red blood cells decreases. The characteristic hematological disorder, the microcytic hypochromic anemia (weakness, low physical and mental performance, decreased heat production, general anemic symptoms, etc.) develops only in long-lasting, severe Fe-deficiency.

- angular stomatitis and fissures, glossitis, cheilosis, watch-glass (concave) nails are the typical symptoms for Fe-deficiency.

- maternal Fe-deficiency is often coupled by premature labor, and small birthweight baby.

- enhanced tendency for Pb-intoxication

- some data suggest that mild Fe deficiency may even be advantageous. It is assumed that it contributes to the relative protection against infarction and stroke in women (lower RBC – decreased viscosity?). In bacterial infections it may also be favorable. Other studies, however, showed some concordance between mild anemia and stroke mortality.

Principles of treating iron deficiency:

Ferrosulphate (300mg/day) orally or in i.m. injections. It causes unpleasant GI side-effects, e.g. constipation, abdominal pain, black stool. The treatment usually lasts long (2-3 months), because the bone marrow is usually hypoplasic and weeks are needed just to start elevation of RBC count. The previously depleted iron stores also have to be re-filled.

Iron intoxication, overdose:

- In the presence of Fe-ions the free radical production increases. In acute intoxication (intake of 200-300 mg/kg) vomiting, diarrhea, cramps, even death may occur. In chronic overdose hemosiderosis develops. Fe deposited in various organs induces tissue damage and fibrosis.

- Hemochromatosis is a hereditary disease with enhanced Fe absorption, in which Fe is deposited and fibrosis develops in the lung, liver, pancreas, heart, joints and the pituitary. It may cause sexual dysfunctions. Incidence: homozygote: 3-4/1000, heterozygote: 1/10!

- Bantu syndrome: In Africa the Bantu people prepare beer in iron boilers. The iron intake is excessive, the cirrhosis and liver cancer are frequent.

FLUORINE (F)

Fluorine plays an important role in normal development of teeth and bones.

Advised daily intake: 0.7-1.2 mg/liter drinking water. The best way of replacement is to assay the fluorine-content of the water and adjust it till the optimal value. Unfortunately, this is expensive, as demonstrated in a trial in Hungary (around Békéscsaba). Fluorine can also be added to salt, or in the period of dental development, it can be applied in tablets (e.g. Dentocar® tablets in Hungary). In case of excessive intake, fluorine is deposited in bones and makes them more rigid and brittle.

EFFECTS OF FLUORINE:

Prevention of caries

- During dental development it is incorporated into the enamel and increases its rigidity.

- Its constant presence (toothpaste) increases remineralization in early phase of caries, when the demineralization is still reversible.

- It inhibits glycolysis (in the mouth, bacterial glycolysis produces acids, which can injure the enamel).

Prevention of osteoporosis

- It enhances osteoblast stimulation, particularly in the continuously re-built

trabecular bones, and enhances the vertebral bone density.

Dangers of excessive fluorine intake:

- Above an intake of 5 mg/kg/day adverse side-effect, at 30-60 mg/kg intake acute toxic symptoms appear: weakness, spasms, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, tear and saliva overproduction. Pulmonary and heart failure may develop.

- Chronic dental fluorosis develops at prolonged intake of 2 mg/liter. Fluorine incorporates into the enamel and causes its discoloration and hypermineralization.

- Chronic bone fluorosis develops upon 10-25 mg/day intake for 7-10 years. Its symptoms: rigidity and pain of joints, long-bone osteosclerosis, exostoses, and the rigid bones are more brittle (neck of femur breaks easily).

- Chronic overdose may also cause muscle atrophy or neurological disorders.

COPPER (Cu)

A number of enzymes need copper as a trace element. It is well absorbed from the duodenum, but easily leaves the body. It is not stored, so, its regular intake is important. The daily need is 2-3 mg. Oysters, shells, meat, oily and other vegetable seeds contain it in high, vegetables in low concentrations.

Functions of copper:

- a component of antioxidants, e.g. superoxide dismutase (SOD), extracellular ceruloplasmin, intracellular copper-thionein

- it is necessary for collagen and elastin synthesis, (e.g. important in wound healing)

- component of the melanin-producing tyrosinase

- necessary for the function of cytochrome oxidase, important in the oxidative phosphorylation

Deficit of copper: leads to anemia, leuko- and neutropenia, hypercholesterolemia, slow wound-healing and ECG abnormalities develop.

Copper intoxication: hepatic steatosis/cirrhosis, disorders of the gastrointestinal and the neural systems.

ZINC (Zn)

Zinc is a cofactor of ca. 200 enzymes, e.g.:

- nucleotide transferase, RNS polymerase

- alkaline phosphatase, carboanhydrase

- SOD (Cu is a part of the catalytic unit, component of the Zn-finger)

- Zn-finger structure is also found in the IC receptors of the steroid hormones, vitamin-A, vitamin-D – thereby it is important in gene expression

- insulin is stored in pancreatic -cells in form of Zn-salt

Symptoms of Zn deficiency:

- dwarfism

- anorexia

- decreased immune functions

- skin lesions

- bone deformities

- decreased reproductive function

- in congenital disorder of Zn absorption acrodermatitis enteropathica and candida albicans infections occur.

Daily need: 10-12 mg. Source: red meats, oyster, shell. Plants also have Zn, but the fitates prevent its absorption.

Excess (over 150 mg/day): dizziness, gastric pain, vomiting.

CHROMIUM (Cr)

It is also called glucose tolerance factor.

Effects:

- enhances the effect of insulin

- promotes growth

- improves immune functions

Daily need: 50-200 g/day. Source: plant seeds, dark chocolate.

Toxicosis (very rare): allergic dermatitis, skin and nasoseptal lesions develop, it enhances the risk of lung cancer.

IODINE (I)

Iodine is a basic component of thyroid hormones (T3-tri-iodothyronine, T4-tetra-iodothyronine). The daily need is the intake of 150 g. Source: see-food. At other places the intake depends on the iodine content of the soil. Far from the see in high mountains (Andes) and the flood areas of rivers, where floods wash out the iodine (including Hungary) the deficiency is frequent. After Fe deficiency, this is the most common deficiency disease, and about 200 million people are affected worldwide. Certain goitrogenes (perchlorate, thiocyanate) inhibit the iodide intake of the thyroid gland. The iodine intake can be secured by adding iodine to the salt.

Deficiency: Moderate iodine deficiency causes normofunctional goiter, severe deficiency leads to hypofunctional goiter. Among people living in iodine-deficient regions congenital iodine deficiency and endemic cretinism develop without iodine supplementation. Function, symptoms: see ch. 10.5.

MANGANESE (Mn)

Mn is constituent of some metalloenzymes. Such is e.g. the Mn-containing SOD, the arginase, the pyruvate carboxylase.

Functions:

- defense against free radicals: antioxidant

- by its role in the ornithine-citrulline cycle, it helps NH3-urea formation in the liver.

- gluconeogenesis

- although not its component, it activates the brain glutamine synthase

Symptoms of deficiency (zoological studies):

- growth retardation

- skeletal abnormalities, e.g. short-thick feet in chicken

- in utero Mn-deficiency causes irreversible ataxia due to disorder of brain development

- perinatal deficiency causes developmental disorder of pancreas, endocrine and exocrine damages

- steatosis hepatis

Daily need: 2-5 mg. Sources: water and air

Intoxication (in contaminated environment the intake is high): Fe-deficiency, growth retardation, cerebral dysfunctions, decreased appetite

SELENIUM (Se)

Se is part of antioxidant enzymes (e.g. glutathione peroxidase). It is also needed for the function of iodothyronine deiodinase, which performs T4-T3 transformation in the periphery.

Deficiency: Indigenous to China, where the commonly known Keshan dilatative cardiomyopathy was found. Its deficiency may induce oxidative damage of other organs (e.g. skeletal muscle). The combined Se+I deficiency causes more severe hypothyroidism than isolated iodine-deficiency does, because the peripheral T4-T3 transformation is also defective.

Daily need: 50-200 g/day.

Intoxication (intake of >30 mg/day was described from food additives in the USA): nausea, vomiting, loss of hair (complete loss within 3-4 days!), nail damage, tiredness, peripheral neuropathy has been described.

A6.4. PRINCIPLES OF ARTIFICIAL/ASSISTED FEEDING

The special medical diet means appropriate adjustments in the quantity, the composition, the way of preparing of the food or in the methods of feeding, according to the actual state of the patient. Quantitative adjustment is e.g. the low-calorie slimming diet in obesity, or intake of extra calories in roborating diets. Qualitative adjustment (changing the composition and consistence) is e.g. in hepatitis the liver-saving diet low in lipids. In chronic gastritis the foodstuff of difficult digestion should be avoided, therefore the way of preparation becomes important (e.g. soft-boiled eggs instead of hard-boiled ones). As regards the method of intake, artificial/clinical feeding is needed if the patient is unable or unwilling to eat spontaneously, or the actual state does not allow oral feeding (e.g. acute pancreatitis). In these cases, readily utilizable nutrients or pre-processed substances are given through the gastrointestinal tract (enteral feeding) or through a venous cannula (parenteral feeding).

Traditionally, diet only prohibited, limited the intake of certain food (negative, restrictive diet), e.g. resticting the intake of some allergenic food. A positive, supplementing diet advises a regulated enhancement in the intake of certain substances (e.g. higher Ca-content of food in postmenopausal osteoporosis). Some diets are life-saving, like the phenylalanine-free diet in PKU, the gluten-free diet in celiac syndrome.

The purpose of the diet may be curative, preventive or palliative. Curative diet is indicated for cure or improvement of the disease, e.g. replacing monovalent essential nutrients (e.g. vitamins, amino acids, minerals), or reducing calorie intake in obesity. The preventive diet serves prevention on a population level. Examples are the iodinated salt, vitamin D enriched milk, and fluorinated water in prevention of goiter, rickets, and caries. The palliative diet does not bring cure, but it can delay further progression of the disease, may even improve the general conditions of the patient (e.g. feeding patients in terminal state, protein-restricted diet in renal or hepatic failure, decreased salt intake in essential hypertension, omission of tyramine-containing cheese from the diet in pheochromocytoma, etc.)

A6.4.1. ENTERAL FEEDING

Indications

Mainly those states, in which the patient is unable or unwilling to consume sufficient amount of food, but the intestinal system is functional. Indications 1) chronic illness (anorexia, cachexia, chronic inflammation), with high need of nutrients while the patient is too weak/anorexic for spontaneous and sufficient oral feeding; 2) cachexia, poor condition, which should be improved e.g. before surgery or in sepsis; 3) after severe burn, trauma or surgery; 4) the oral feeding is impossible for technical reasons (e.g. swallowing problems, injuries of the neck, jaw, oral cavity, esophagus); 5) feeding through enteral cannula may help the adaptation of the gastrointestinal tract in premature or small babies.

Enteral feeding is contraindicated in shock, ileus, peritonitis, enteriris, lasting gastrointestinal bleedings, or in case of too short bowel (less than 30 cm).

Methods of enteral feeding

Additional per os feeding is necessary, if the amount or quality of the consumed food is not sufficient, but with additional mashy food rich in calories and nutrients it may suffice the actual needs.

Nasogastric or nasojejunal probes: special fluid nutrients are injected by a thin, soft (PVC, polyurethane, silicone) nutritional probe introduced through a nostril into the stomach or the jejunum. With disorders of swallowing reflex, the probe may end in the bronchi, therefore the proper location must always be confirmed (suction of fluid, auscultation, X-ray). The probe must be exchanged regularly, but the method can be used for weeks or months. Probe-feeding may be additional (some nutrient component is given this way) to oral feeding, or may be total enteral feeding. Besides supplying energy/nutrients, the method can also be applied for introducing unpleasant drugs, minerals or special food-additives (e.g. in steatorrhea medium-size fatty acids can be mixed to the nutrients, they are absorbed without bile acids).

Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostoma (PEG) or jejunostoma (PEJ), or if the percutaneous implantation is impossible (e.g. due to tumor), surgical jejunostomy can be prepared, in all cases when severe lesion of the upper parts of the GI tract (tumor, oropharyngeal surgery, neurological difficulties of swallowing, tracheostomy, etc.), severe nausea, risk of pulmonary aspiration are present and the probe cannot be introduced safely through the nostril, but at least 3-4 weeks-long nutrition is required. Surgical fistulas may also be prepared on temporary basis, by these the required nutrients can be introduced into the GI system.

Complications of enteral feeding are: possible occlusion of the thin probe, irritation by the probe, or infection.

A6.4.2. PARENTERAL NUTRITION

Indications

In parenteral nutrition glucose-solution, amino acid mixture, or lipid emulsion is given intravenously (circumventing the GI system) supplemented with electrolytes, trace elements, vitamins. Most important indications are those intestinal diseases, in which an intestinal section is lacking or its transient dysfunction necessitates its „rest” as therapy (e.g. in severe malabsorptions, GI surgery, ulcerative colitis, Crohn disease, intestinal fistula, short bowel syndrome, pancreatitis, protracted ileus, volvulus, invagination). It may be applied in patients who are not able (coma) or willing to eat due to anorexia, nausea/vomiting (e.g. in renal- or hepatic failure, where low-protein, but appropriate amino acid composition is necessary with careful control of electrolyte balance. As a supplementary treatment it may be used in states of reconvalescence from serious disease, or in lasting hypermetabolic states, in which the patient is unable to eat enough (postoperative and posttraumatic states, preparation for surgery, and cachectic tumor patients before the side-effects of chemotherapy or irradiation).

Methods of parenteral nutrition

Parenteral nutrition may be partial, supplementary (e.g. beside enteral artificial feeding) and it may be the only nutrient source of the patient, i.e. total parenteral nutrition (TPN). In lasting TPN the GI system may atrophize rather quickly, therefore nutrition should be followed by the enteral ways as soon as possible. The quantity of parenteral nutrients is to be decreased gradually until enteral or oral intake of sufficient energy can be secures (combined nutrition).

Cannula implanted to a peripheral or central vein (superior v. cava, external or internal jugular vein, subclavic vein) is used for this type of nutrition. Peripheral veins can be used for partial and short-lasting (< 7 days) nutrition: although this is simpler and the technical complications of central cannulation can be avoided, it has to be exchanged frequently, and the danger of osmotic injury, phlebitis, thrombus formation and catheter-sepsis is rather high. TPN for months, years can be performed through central veins, which allow giving substance sin higher concentrations. Its early dangers are technical and connected with the implantation: embolism, hemothorax, pneumothorax, hydrothorax, chylothotax, injury of blood- or lymphatic-vessels, injury of the thoracic duct, etc. Obstruction, thrombosis, infections, even sepsis may occur even later on. The late metabolic complications are as follows:

hypo/hyperglycemia, hyperosmolarity

disorders of electrolyte- and fluid-balance (e.g. edema)

disorders of acid-base balance (e.g. hyperglycemic ketoacidosis, hyperchloremic acidosis)

disorders of nitrogen-balance

liver damage (steatosis, hyperbilirubinemia, cholestasis, cholelithiasis, etc.)

osteomalacia, osteoporosis

gastrointestinal atrophy

hypertriglyceridemia, essential fatty acid deficiency, etc.

In order to avoid these complications, the precise composition of the nutrient solutions and added electrolytes must be established according to the actual needs of the patient, and it must be modified according to data of constant monitoring (e.g. in heart failure the volume overload has to be avoided; in respiratory failure less CO2 should be produced, therefore lipid-emulsion covers the non-protein calories).

The basic constituents of TPN: water (30-40 ml/kg/day), calories according to need (30-60 kcal/kg/day), amino acids according to the catabolism (1-3 g/kg/day). The peripheral veins allow infusion of isotonic 5% or often 10% glucose, more concentrated (20-40%) can only be given centrally. This was particularly important before the introduction of lipid emulsions for calorie replacement. With the glucose, solutions of protein-hydrolizates or crystalline amino acids should be given, 50% of which has to be essential amino acid plus other amino acids serving as non-specific N-source to help optimal utilization. For energy replacement fatty acids can be infused since a milk-like, non-toxic emulsion was prepared from soybean-oil and egg-yolk-lecithin mixture (in Hungary: Intralipid®). To avoid fat-embolism, lipid emulsions can only be given centrally, they have to contain essential PUFA (in its absence dermatitis develops within weeks). In longer TPN the vitamin and mineral supplies have to be secured: antibiotic destruction of the gut bacterial flora induces vitamin K deficiency and bleedings within 2 weeks; the usual solutions do not always contain enough phosphate (within 7-10 days the ATP and 2,3-DPG may be deficient), nor Zn (anemia, splenomegaly, in babies: growth retardation, later hypogonadism and slow wound healing), nor Cr (it is followed by diabetoid glucose tolerance). Vitamin-A deficiency may develop for technical reasons: it may precipitate on the wall of the sack containing the nutrient solution.

Monitoring parenteral nutrition

Continuous monitoring is necessary to avoid the effects and side-effects of parenteral nutrition:

daily measurement of body weight, se-glucose and -triglyceride levels, the control of electrolyte- and fluid-balance, the N-balance (se-urea, se-creatinine, urinary urea), and the blood count and blood gases.

Every 3rd day control of hepatic functions, se-protein, Ca, Mg and PO4-levels, prothrombine time, osmolarity in serum and urine.

Every 2nd week assessment of the nutritional state and the complement level.

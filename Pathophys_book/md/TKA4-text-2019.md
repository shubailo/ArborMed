A.4. PHYSICAL ACTIVITY, INACTIVITY, IMMOBILIZATION

In affluent societies the general level of physical activity progressively decreases. This is unfortunate, since physical activity has many advantages regarding physiological functions. In Hungary, in 2014 only 26% of adults did some sport of at least 2.5-h/week. In some diseases, however, physical activity is limited, even immobilization is possible.

A.4.1. PHYSICAL ACTIVITY

Beneficial effects of physical activity:

- Enhancement of energy dissipation, it helps to reach or maintain normal, healthy body mass. In active athletes, for example, the usual body weight rise of middle-aged persons is absent. Regular physical activity contributes to prevention (or decrease) of obesity.

- Regular sporting improves body composition, it increases the amount of muscle mass, the ratio of active tissues, attenuates their age-dependent natural loss. Training programs may be able to increase the available muscle mass by 10% even in old ages. The greater ratio of active tissues enhances BMR. Training can prevent the deterioration of body composition (loss of proteins) induced by yo-yo syndrome.

- The trained muscle, during prolonged work (>15-20 min) burns fat. On the surface of muscle fibers lipoprotein-lipase (LPL) appears, which is able to split fatty acids from the circulating lipoproteins. With training programs, the amount of type-I, slow, red fibers increases.

- The active skeletal muscles are able to take up glucose by an insulin-independent mechanism. The physical activity pulls the GLUT 4 glucose transporter molecules to the surface of muscle cells, just as insulin does. Thus, regular activity decreases the insulin need, attenuates the load on β-cells, and helps to prevent 2DM.

- Regular physical activity suppresses the total-cholesterol level, but elevates the serum HDL-cholesterol concentration.

- The thermal adaptation capability improves. Trained persons can better adapt to either warm or cold environments – with decreasing level of training (e.g. aging) the adaptability is inhibited in both directions.

- In working muscles adrenaline leads to vasodilation, thereby it decreases the total peripheral resistance (TPR). Accordingly, at the onset of physical exercise in healthy persons the diastolic pressure slightly decreases or stagnates, and shows a slow rise only later. The systolic pressure rises from the beginning of work, and during strong exercise it may rise a lot (with training this rise is smaller). Regular exercise helps preventing the development of essential hypertension, which would rely to a great extent on a high TPR. In untrained persons the systolic pressure increases quickly, but the diastolic pressure also rises.

- Regular sports in children and young persons by adulthood increases peak bone mass by applying a load on the bones (piezoelectric effect). The regular physical activity shifts bone metabolism to greater synthesis even at later ages. Regular sports can help in the prevention of osteoporosis (ch. 10.6.4.), which carries the dangers of vertebral compression, pathological fractures, fracture of the hip or the neck of the femur.

- Physical activity has an effect to decrease stress. It has no negative side-effects as e.g. those side-effects that alcohol or sedatives have, and does not evoke pathological dependence, either.

- According to human surveys, regular physical activity can attenuate the appearance of depression and dementia that often come with advancing age. Data obtained in the mouse-model of experimental Alzheimer disease have shown that an appropriate increase of physical activity positively influences the level of brain-derived neural growth factor (BDNF) (the lack of which is a contributing factor in hippocampal atrophy, and also in the amyloid formation).

- The physically active lifestyle decreases the occurrence of certain cancers (colon, breast, uterus, esophagus, prostate) partly by maintaining normal body weight, partly through humoral factors. It influences insulin sensitivity, the level of insulin-like growth factors, and probably the ratio of estrogen/estrogen-binding-globulin.

On the basis of all these, the question arises, why the doctors do not prescribe regular physical activity against visceral obesity, insulin-resistant diabetes, hypertension disease, hypercholesterolemia (i.e. the deadly 4 of metabolic X syndrome), or for the prevention of osteoporosis, extreme stress, depression, even Alzheimer-type dementia? Why governments and insurance companies do not provide bigger efforts and more money for projects aiming these problems, why is sport not better advertised? Physical activity is a type of behavior and not a drug for selling. Sport is not the interest of pharmaceutical companies (or governments?) it is the interest of the individual.

Surveys show that minimum 3x1-h/week intensive sport, or in the elderly daily half-hour moderate physical activity (3xRMR), faster walks can reach significant improvement. Young people with daily 1-1.2 hours of intensive physical activity can maintain a normal body weight without any special diet.

According to a recent study, in healthy young university students a 2-3-weeks long moderate bicycle-ergometric training (60 min sessions 3 times a week, reaching 50-60% of maximal oxygen consumption) had beneficial cardiovascular effects. These effects were still present for about 2 weeks after finishing the training program.

A.4.2. INACTIVITY

The propagation of motor vehicles, television, video, DVD, computers, internet, etc., as well as the automatization at workplaces and homes, all decrease the level of general physical activity.

In the 80-es UK surveys showed that the risk of obesity was 5-fold in children watching TV for 5-h/day vs. 2-h/day. These children start an inactive life at a very early age. Another survey on 3 and 5 year-old children found that the average energy need in the 3-y group was 56% above the RMR, while in th5 5-y group this value was 61%. Children of the 2 groups spent 78-76% of their times with rest, and only 2-4% with real physical activity.

In the USA amongst teenagers a follow-up study in young subjects between 9-10 to 19-20 years of age demonstrated that the BMI of the active subjects (ca. 30%) was 2.1 (Caucasian) or 2.9 (Afro-American) kg/m2 lower than in the inactive counterparts (29%), the differences being 4-6 kg (Caucasian) or 6-9 kg (Afro-American). The skinfold thickness indicated similar fat tissue differences. The physical activity was expressed in MET/week (1 MET* = 3.6 ml/kg/min), e.g. 5 MET intensity work performed 4 times a week for 0.5 years (5x4x0.5) = 10 MET/week (the length of individual performances is not shown in this calculation). In these youngsters the physical activity significantly decreased with age from 28-40 MET/week (active + inactive) to 4-30 in Caucasians and to 3-22 in Afro-Americans.

A.4.3. IMMOBILIZATION SYNDROME – CHRONIC BED-REST

During a night-time sleep 4-8 hours are spent in horizontal position resting in bed. In the course of some diseases we may be forced to „stay in bed” for a couple of days. In such cases the short-time bed rest has some advantages: this rest decreases the burden of the cardiovascular and the respiratory systems.

*MET: metabolic unit. At rest the oxygen consumption of a healthy adult is ca. 250 ml/min. This is relatively standard in sportsmen and sedentary people alike. If this value is referred to body weight (250 ml/min / 70 kg) the received MET value is about 3.6 ml/kg/min. In the last century chronic bed-rest was very often used and for relatively long times:

- following AMI, for example, 6-weeks complete bed-rest was compulsory,

- in fractures of lower extremities several months (hip fracture: 6 months) bed-rest was advocated.

There was a conference already in the 60-es titled ”Abuse-like application of bed-rest in medicine”. At the end of 90-es, several meta-analyses (e.g. Lancet 1999, J. Clin. Epidemiol. 2003) demonstrated that chronic bed-rest is rather harmful than advantageous. It has been proven that a 2-12-days „short” bed-rest after AMI carries no more complications than 5-28-days „longer” bed-rests. Today the American College of Cardiology and the American Heart Association in cases of simple, non-complicated AMI advise at least 12-hours bed-rest.

Some patients cannot avoid chronic immobilization:

- Loss of both legs (or sometimes one), although artificial legs may be used

- Paralysis of the lower half of the body due to transversal lesion of the spinal cord

- Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS, gradually full paralysis develops: the patient continuously needs assisted ventilation): Steven Hawking, a philosopher from Cambridge, lived for decades in this state, he communicated with his environment by minimal finger-movements and a computer. ALS patients, if artificially ventilated and carefully nursed, may stay alive for 10-15 years, they have no mental damage.

- Late stage of Parkinson disease

- Neuropathies (e.g. diabetic, alcoholic, uremic, B12 deficiency) in late stages

- Stroke-induced paralysis

- Immobilization because of coma

- Extreme weakness, severe pains in the joins, chronic diseases (e.g. chronic heart failure, COPD), extreme obesity, rheumatic polymyalgia, hypothyroidism

- Mainly in elderly: depression, isolation, fear of falling, dementia*

- Drugs: sedatives, narcotics (because of sedative effect), diuretics, antihypertensive medication (in the elderly they may cause orthostatic hypotension, dizziness).

Circulatory adaptation to chronic bed-rest

Normally, in orthostatic position a significant part of the circulating blood is congested in the veins of the lower half of the body. To counterbalance this (blood pressure would decrease – the baroreceptor reflex from the carotid sinus and the aortic arch are activated), the sympathetic tone increases the venous return (Fig. 2.13.) and the enhancement of RAAS activity increases the circulating blood volume and cardiac output.

In prolonged supine position (or in weightlessness) the circulation is rearranged, the central regions receive about 700-900 ml more blood. On the short term the central blood volume increases.

Muscle wasting

Muscle contractures develop (muscles and joints are less moveable). In case if upper extremities are affected, the elderly loses capability to eat alone, in case of lower extremities one contracture is enough to cause full immobility. Regular passive movements of joints may help to prevent contracture (although at the beginning it may be painful).

A decrease of muscle mass can be observed already on the short-term, but upon a long-term bed-rest it is significantly enhanced. In the course of 1-2 months of inactivity the muscle amount is halved, particularly the lower extremities and the back are affected. The N-excretion – showing protein catabolism – is significantly enhanced.

The enhanced excretion of hydroxy-proline is a sign of increased muscle (protein) catabolism, while the Ca++-excretion refers to bone absorption.

Decreased physical performance

The factors influencing the capacity of responding to physical loads is negatively modified by bed-rest. The maximal capacity for physical work (VO2max) will not be determined by the capacity of the cardiovascular system, but by the exhaustion of the muscular system, or lack of local substrates (glycogen).

The muscle mass is decreased and the muscles are severely deconditioned. In skeletal muscles the glycogen content decreases, the muscle fibers contain less mitochondria. The amount of functioning capillaries is smaller (therefore the distance for diffusion from capillaries to muscle fiber is longer), the capillary blood perfusion and hydrostatic pressure decrease. The lessened, deconditioned muscles of poor perfusion van take up less oxygen from the blood, therefore the oxygen extraction is low, the venous oxygen content is relatively high, the a-v O2 difference is small.

Formation of red blood cells decreases and the red blood cell number is low. The low level of total ventilation (VEmax) also lessens the amount of oxygen carried by the arterial blood, although the increasing V/Q and the good diffusion in the pulmonary capillaries act against this.

The decreased blood volume, decreased muscle tone and mass (decreased filling) and decreased baroreceptor response act to suppress stroke volume. In contrast, the low adrenergic vasoconstriction (TPR↓) and enhanced -adrenergic activity (enhanced contractility) acts rather to increase stroke volume.

From the modulators of heart rate the decreased baroreceptor sensitivity acts to decrease, the enhanced sympathetic tone to increase the number of contractions. Following chronic bed-rest physical activity evokes an exaggerated cardiovascular response, e.g. palpitation may appear already at work of low oxygen consumption.

Pressure ulcer (decubitus)

At the points exposed to pressure the skin and the deeper tissues may be damaged in course of prolonged sitting or lying. Typical points: nape, ear, shoulder-blade, vertebrae/ribs, sacrum, hip (trochanter maior and sacroiliac joint), elbow, knee, ankle, heel, big toe, and contacting wet surface areas (e.g. region of genitals).

The prevalence of pressure ulcer is 29% among elderly patients who are bed-ridden or have to stay in a wheel-chair for at least a week. Every further week adds to it 7-8%. The pressure ulcers usually develop during the first two weeks of stay in a hospital. More than half of pressure ulcers develop in patient aged over 70, but they may appear in young athletic persons as well, in case of lasting coma state.

Contributing factors to the development of pressure ulcer:

- Immobilization: in patients spontaneously changing position at least 50-times per night pressure ulcer never occurred, in those with less than 20 position changes pressure ulcer developed in 90%.

- Fecal-, urinary-incontinence: Vapor macerates the skin, the pressure points (and contact points) are particularly sensitive, substances and bacteria of the feces cause extra irritation and damage.

- Hypalbuminemia: In poorly fed, protein-deficient patients the regeneration tendency of the skin and the tissues is lower therefore pressure ulcers develop more easily.

Pathomechanism of pressure ulcers:

Pressure: A pressure of already 60-70 mmHg may cause muscle fiber degeneration within 1-2-h. This leads to development of deep subcutaneous vesicles (like skin blisters from a new shoe). The covering skin may even appear normal. In case of a usual patient on a normal hospital bed the pressure over the bigger trochanter or sacral bone may reach 100-150 mmHg. This pressure blocks the capillary perfusion, and the transcutaneous oxygen pressure decreases to zero. The extravasation of fluid from the capillaries increases, the diffusion way in the tissues become longer, and the supply of cells with nutrients and oxygen further deteriorates. In edematous tissues relatively small pressure may cause decubitus. The primary problem is the disorder of capillary and lymphatic flow. With cooling the pressure-induced damage may be somewhat attenuated. The injury of subcutaneous tissues contributes to the skin lesions in the presence of pressure.

Shearing stress and friction: The shearing stress is particularly pronounced if the back of the patient is propped up by over 30º and the patient keeps sliding down. Adherence of gluteal tissues prevents slipping down completely, but the gluteal vessels are stretched in the meantime and they become anatomically distorted. Shearing stress develops with the incompetent turning/moving of the patient.

Vapor: In urinary incontinent patients the probability of developing a decubitus is 5.5-times higher than in non-incontinent ones.

Stages of pressure ulcer:

1. Lasting (non-suppressible) erythema/discoloration on skin surface.

2. Clear-cut superficial wound, which does not reach subcutaneous tissues.

3. Deep wound, affecting the subcutaneous tissues. It does not cross the fascia of			the muscle.

4. Very deep wound, battering also the muscles, bones, joints. It may have 	tunnel-like extensions into the surrounding tissues.

Consequences of pressure ulcer:

Decubitus causes 4-fold increase in the mortality rate of the patients. Non-curing pressure ulcers in old-people’s homes lead to 6-fold increase in mortality.

Bacterial infection of the ulcers is very frequent, and among them anaerobic bacteria can often be found (the strong smell indicates this). The infection may reach the bone and may cause osteomyelitis. In severe cases the cause of death is sepsis.

Prevention and treatment of pressure ulcer:

To prevent immobilization and pressure-injuries the patients have to be moved, turned, the position must be changed at least in every 2nd hour. Sidewise maximum 30º elevation is allowed, once in the left, once in the right direction. With greater elevations the pressure at pressure points increases too much.

Special mattress can be used to decrease the pressure load. Care is needed with the use of special decubitus-cushions with hole in the middle, because they may compress vessels in the surrounding areas and the perfusion of just in the injured region may deteriorate.

Pneumonia

The ventilation decreases, the V/Q mismatching becomes pronounced, and the mucociliary clearance of the airways becomes insufficient. Elderly patient bed-ridden for only a couple of days may develop hypostatic (congestive) pneumonia („hypostatic” means that in the airways of the dorsal/lower parts exudates accumulate, the regional capillaries are also congested). Since this usually happens in hospitals, the infection is nosocomial, the treatment is difficult. Surgical fixation of a fractured neck of femur is indicated mainly by speeding up mobilization and avoiding the pneumonia mortality induced by long (earlier 9 weeks) bed-rest.

Pulmonary embolism

When longer bed-rest proves unavoidable, Fraxiparine® (low molecular weight heparin) is given, in order to prevent the development of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. The deep venous thrombosis is due mainly to the decreased venous return, but the fall in plasma volume and the consequent hemoconcentration also contribute to it. Diagnosis of both the deep venous thrombosis and the pulmonary (incomplete) embolism is rather difficult. In the elderly population they enhance mortality by about 50%.

Obstipation

Immobilization decreases intestinal motility. The tendency for constipation increases significantly, even impactation may happen, eventually with consequent fecal incontinence.

Metabolism - thermoregulation

In chronic bed-rest the metabolic rate may be 22% lower than normally. The level of body temperature is also shifted down (some data show a decrease of 0.7 ºC). The circadian rhythm of body temperature exhibits abnormal patterns, mainly the amplitudes decrease.

The defense against both heat and cold is weaker. The human thermoregulation relies mainly on behavioral functions, but the immobilized patients have little chance for this. However, the efficacy of the autonomic defense is also minimal. In a cold environment the rise of metabolic rate/heat production is limited, in warm environments the skin perfusion and sweating may not be able to increase sufficiently. Even passive physical training can enhance cold- and warm-tolerances (!).

Immune system

The activity of the immune system may show some decrease. Most observations showed stability of leukocyte numbers (although the high cortisol level may decrease their efficiency). In some cases, decreased IgA and IgG production was found.

Bones - osteoporosis

Even in a young athlete, a 4-months bed-rest may decrease bone mass by about 1.4%. This is an irreversible change, since the lost bone mass cannot be regained even either a 6-months intensive training. The net increase of bone mass can be observed exclusively in children, in adult only the extent and speed of loss can be influenced. Immobilization greatly enhances the progression of pre-existing osteoporosis in elderly patients.
